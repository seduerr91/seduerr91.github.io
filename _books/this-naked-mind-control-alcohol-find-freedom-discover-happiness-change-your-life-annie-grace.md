## Hook

This Naked Mind offers a revolutionary path to freedom from alcohol by dismantling the subconscious beliefs and societal conditioning that drive our dependence, replacing willpower with understanding.

## Overview

Annie Grace combines neuroscience, psychology, and personal experience to challenge the cultural myths surrounding alcohol. By addressing the root causes of our drinking habits rather than the symptoms, this book guides readers toward a state of spontaneous sobriety where the desire to drink simply disappears.

## Summary

### The Naked Mind

Annie Grace presents a compelling solution for those worried that drinking is affecting their health but unwilling to seek change due to the misery and stigma often associated with recovery. *This Naked Mind* offers a path to freedom that does not rely on scare tactics, pain, strict rules, or a lifetime of struggle. Instead, it addresses the underlying causes of alcohol dependence to provide a permanent solution. By weaving together psychological, neurological, cultural, social, and industry factors, Grace opens the reader's eyes to the startling role alcohol plays in our culture and our lives.

#### The Role of the Subconscious

The central premise of the book is that our relationship with alcohol is dictated by our subconscious mind. While we may make a conscious decision to stop drinking, we often experience cognitive dissonance because our subconscious still holds conflicting beliefs. Grace defines cognitive dissonance as the mental stress experienced when holding two contradictory values or ideas simultaneously.

The subconscious mind forms our perception of reality based on personal experiences, observations, assumptions, and conclusions. It has been conditioned by society, advertising, and culture to view alcohol as a reward, a tool for relaxation, or a social lubricant. To change our behavior, we must first change these foundational beliefs. By aligning the subconscious brain with the conscious desire to quit, readers can achieve what Grace calls "spontaneous sobriety"-a state where the desire to drink disappears without the need for willpower.

#### Dismantling the Great Alcohol Myth

Grace, leveraging her background as an advertising executive, delves into the influence of media and culture in perpetuating what she calls the "Great Alcohol Myth." She explains how the alcohol industry exploits human desires, marketing their products to make us appear smarter, wealthier, more refined, and sexier. This pervasive marketing convinces us that alcohol is indispensable for enjoyment, relaxation, and social success.

However, Grace contends that alcohol is actually robbing us of the very things it promises to deliver. She argues that alcohol does not make us funny, brave, or happy; rather, it undermines our well-being and health. By exposing the contrast between the marketing myth and the reality of drinking's effects, she aims to strip alcohol of its power. This method, described as "unconscious conditioning" through negative association, works to rewire the brain's response to alcohol.

#### Redefining Addiction and Habit

Grace challenges conventional definitions of alcoholism. She asserts that over time, with the right level of exposure, anyone can develop a physical dependence on alcohol. She rejects the binary view that separates "alcoholics" from "normal" drinkers, suggesting instead that we are all susceptible and perhaps on a slow slide into addiction once we begin to drink.

She frames alcohol dependence primarily as a habitual and emotional dependency rather than strictly a chemical and physical addiction. While some critics argue she under-emphasizes the chemical "buzz" that drives excess, Grace maintains that understanding the brain's learning process is key. She points to the statistic that 75 percent of people who recover from alcohol dependence do so without professional help, validating her approach that internal cognitive shifts can be more powerful than external interventions.

#### The Practice of Liminal Thinking

A core tool in the book is "Liminal Thinking." This practice involves the conscious exploration and acceptance of new truths to influence the unconscious mind. Grace includes 'Liminal Points' throughout the text-questions and prompts designed to challenge ingrained beliefs. These include thought-provoking inquiries such as "Is drinking a habit?" "Are we really drinking for the taste?" and "Does drinking make me happy?"

By honestly answering these questions, readers can expose the false premises that support their drinking. This process restores the ability to make rational and logical decisions about alcohol, no longer influenced by illogical or irrational desires. The goal is not just moderation or abstinence, but the freedom to choose one's role with alcohol without any sense of deprivation.

#### Freedom, Not Deprivation

Ultimately, *This Naked Mind* is about reclaiming joy and agency. Grace insists that giving up alcohol should not feel like a loss but a gain. She argues that we often look to alcohol because we feel deficient, believing we need a substance to compensate for what we lack. However, she emphasizes that we already possess the vitality and happiness we seek.

The book advises readers to continue drinking while they read, avoiding the anxiety and restriction that often fuel cravings. It suggests that by doing the work of examining beliefs, one can eventually reach a point where, like Grace herself, they can drink as much as they want simply because they no longer want to. Whether one chooses moderation or complete sobriety, the book offers a roadmap to a self-empowered, healthier existence grounded in awareness and truth rather than habit and myth.

   This book is a total game-changer for those looking to cultivate a mindful relationship with alcohol.
“This Naked Mind” is of particular benefit to those wishing to limit alcohol intake, although author Annie Grace makes it clear that giving up the sauce is not a requirement.
She gives you the facts and empowers you to choose for yourself the role that alcohol will play in your life.
I had the pleasure of meeting Annie Grace at an author event at a Colorado bookstore, The Bookworm of Edwards, and it was an honor to hear about the inspiration for writing this book — her personal experience and subsequent success with sobriety after years of alcohol abuse.
She credits her change in perspective to Dr. John Sarno, whom she worked with to cure her issues with chronic back pain.
Dr. Sarno’s approach investigates the mind-body connection and implements practices to address the deepest root-cause of an affliction rather than simply remedying symptoms alone.
Her success with this methodology got her wondering if this approach could be successful with addressing alcohol abuse.
She used herself as the “guinea pig” and found tremendous success.
This was the initial impetus for her writing and publishing “This Naked Mind: Control Alcohol, Find Freedom, Discover Happiness & Change Your Life.”
The subconscious mind is said to be the driving force behind desires.
Although we can make the conscious decision not to drink, without addressing our subconscious beliefs and desires, therein lies cognitive dissonance.

   Annie Grace opens her 2015 self-help book about alcohol use by asking a provocative question:
*“What if, by reversing years of unconscious conditioning, you could return to the perspective of a non-drinker?”*
Grace proceeds to talk readers through her strategy to return to a mental state where the desire for a drink disappears.
She blends snippets from her personal struggle with alcohol abuse with a barrage of statistics, facts and perspectives about the dangers of drinking.
Her bold opinions challenge societal norms and accepted beliefs about alcohol.
For starters, she takes issue with the view that some people have an addiction to alcohol and need to quit, while others are not addicted and may therefore proceed without reflection:
*“It seems strange to use the term ‘alcoholic.’
We don’t have cigarette-o-holics but rather people who have smoked and therefore become addicted to cigarettes.
Similarly, you don’t hear about cocaine addicts suffering from cocaineism.
If you consider yourself a regular drinker, you probably take issue with this sentiment.
Why?
Because if we agree that no specific, diagnosable physical defect separates alcoholics from the population of “responsible” drinkers, everyone who drinks is susceptible and perhaps on the path to alcohol dependence.
I assert that over time, with the right level of exposure, anyone can develop a physical dependence on alcohol.”*
Grace takes the provocative stance that anyone who drinks any amount of alcohol has some degree of dependence and therefore risks eventual addiction.
*“Consider the possibility that since we are human, and since alcohol is addictive to humans, once we begin to drink we unconsciously begin the slow slide into addiction.”
* Her writing reads as if she is giving each reader a personal pep talk by touting the reader’s ability to change their lives through re-wiring their unconscious, hard-wired urge to drink.
Liminal Thinking is the centerpiece of Grace’s strategy:
*“Liminal Thinking defines how, through the conscious exploration and acceptance of new truths, you can influence your unconscious mind.
This gives you back your ability to make rational and logical decisions about alcohol, no longer influenced by illogical, emotional, or irrational desires.
It will give you control and freedom by changing your understanding of and therefore your relationship with alcohol.
While tradition, advertising and societal norms condition our unconscious to believe that alcohol is beneficial, Liminal Thinking and the material in this book with expose that unconscious conditioning and recondition your unconscious, exposing alcohol and giving you freedom.”*
In other words, This Naked Mind functions as a barrage of fascinating, engaging albeit unsettling information about alcohol aimed at jolting the reader into a new space of consciousness.
Her idea is that the very process of making one’s self read the book is a form of negative conditioning that can generate rewiring of unconscious associations that shape the desire to drink and thus pave the road to addiction.
Is she arguing for a dry society?
Maybe.
Does she make a strong case?
Many readers believe she does.
While she offers some space for modest alcohol use, the wiggle room is negligible:
*“Even if you drink moderately, it is vital to be aware that your drinking is most likely increasing, not decreasing, over time.
There are some exceptions.
Let’s take my friend Todd.
He approaches alcohol with extreme caution, and for this reason he allows himself a single beer on Friday and a single beer on Saturday.
He never drinks more than one, and he doesn’t drink on other occasions.
He is strict with these rules, and it is his way of ensuring addiction does not take over.
He will probably, as long as he maintains his level of staunch self-control, go his entire life without developing a physical dependence.
That being said, Todd’s body is building a tolerance to even those single beers, and the actual effect he believes them to have is probably nonexistent.
While it’s great to have this level of self-control, the majority of us are not like Todd.”*
The book was brought to my attention by a therapy client whose relationship with alcohol was transformed by Grace’s words.
Having read the book and suggested it to others, several clients describe This Naked Mind as a powerful, memorable read.
Grace does not celebrate Alcoholics Anonymous, treatment programs, therapy, or medication.
