## Hook

Peter Hessler drives through the heart of China's transformation, revealing how the rise of the automobile and the expansion of roads are reshaping a nation from the isolated farms of the north to the bustling factories of the south.

## Overview

Country Driving chronicles the human side of China's economic revolution through three interconnected journeys: a road trip along the Great Wall, life in a village undergoing rapid modernization, and the rise of an industrial town. Hessler illustrates how infrastructure development, particularly the highway boom, acts as a catalyst for massive social change, driving migration and altering individual livelihoods. The book

## Summary

### Introduction

In 2001, Peter Hessler acquired a Chinese driver's license, an act that granted him entry into a brave new world. At the time, Beijing was adding nearly a thousand new drivers every day, marking the dawn of a nationwide auto boom. For the growing middle class, a car represented mobility, prosperity, and modernity. For Hessler, it meant adventure. Over the next six years, he traveled across the country, using the road network as a lens to observe the human side of China's economic transformation. The result is *Country Driving*, a narrative divided into three sections that track the nation's shift from a rural, isolated society to an urban, interconnected manufacturing powerhouse.

### Book I: The Wall

The first section of the book chronicles Hessler's 7,000-mile road trip across northern China, following the Great Wall from the East China Sea to the Tibetan plateau. This region, once the cultural heartland, is rapidly being drained of its lifeblood. Hessler drives through landscapes defined by loneliness, describing villages that are impoverished and significantly depopulated. He encounters a demographic reality skewed heavily toward the elderly and very young children, as the working-age generation has migrated to the southeast in search of employment.

This journey highlights the stark contrast between the historical efforts to keep the world out-symbolized by the Wall-and the modern rush to connect through roads. Hessler picks up hitchhikers who tell him there is "nothing for me back home." By observing these abandoned communities, Hessler documents the "other side" of the greatest migration in human history. He illustrates that the massive shift of population is not just about overcrowded factory cities, but also about the desolate villages left behind, where the future often seems bleak.

### Book II: The Village

In the second section, Hessler narrows his focus to Sancha, a remote farming village north of Beijing. When he first arrives, the village is accessible only by a dead-end dirt road. However, a government road-building campaign soon paves the route, and the capital's auto boom brings a wave of tourists to the mountains. This sudden connectivity disrupts the quiet rhythm of village life.

Hessler rents a farmhouse and lives beside the Wei family, observing their transformation over six years. Wei Ziqi, a reluctant farmer, attempts to remake himself as a middle-class entrepreneur to cater to the new visitors. The narrative captures the complexities of this transition. The family's income rises, but they face new challenges, including a frightening health crisis involving their son and the lack of rural healthcare. As traditional social structures breakdown, the family must navigate new economic realities. Hessler explores the concept of *guanxi*, or personal connections, which becomes essential for doing business. Amidst the chaos, the villagers often employ the philosophy of *mei banfa*, or "nothing can be done," to accept their hardships and move on.

### Book III: The Factory

The final section shifts to the industrial zone of Lishui, a small city in southeastern Zhejiang province. Here, officials hope a new government-built expressway will transform an agricultural region into a major industrial center. Hessler spends three years researching this development, telling the city's story through the lens of a single factory that produces nylon-coated metal rings for bra straps.

Through this manufacturing microcosm, Hessler documents the relentless drive of development. He introduces the bosses, the entrepreneurs, and the migrant workers who fuel the boom. He details the financial mechanisms behind this growth, where cities convert rural land to urban use to sell development rights, pouring billions into infrastructure to attract factories. The section reveals the high stakes of this game-industrial espionage, death threats, and the constant pressure to succeed. It paints a portrait of a society tearing off into the future with only the sketchiest of maps, driven by individual desires for a better life rather than by a grand, centralized plan.

### Conclusion

*Country Driving* is fundamentally a book about people. While it contains statistics and incisive analysis of policies, its core strength lies in its portrayal of ordinary lives during extraordinary times. Hessler shows that China's transformation is not merely the result of top-down mandates, but the aggregate of millions of individual choices. From the farmers abandoning their land to the villagers turning their homes into guesthouses, and the workers migrating to factory towns, the Chinese people areshrugging off the weight of history and adapting to a new world. By focusing on the tangible details of specific lives and places, Hessler provides a grounded explanation for why China looks the way it does today, offering a deeply human account of a historic economic shift.

   How do you drive in China?
In 2001, when Peter Hessler applied for a Chinese driver’s license, he entered a brave new world.
In Beijing alone, almost a thousand drivers registered on average each day, the pioneers of a nationwide auto boom.
Most of them came from the growing middle class, for whom a car represented mobility, prosperity, modernity.
For Hessler, it meant adventure.
...
With license in hand, Hessler spent the next six years researching the final book in his China trilogy.
He began the series with
*River Town*, which explored the theme of geography in a fast-changing country, and then, with *Oracle Bones*, he examined the role of history in today’s modern society.
In *Country Driving*, he turns his attention to development, capturing the human side of China’s economic transformation.
Through three distinct stories,
*Country Driving* tracks the rise of the automobile in Chinese life.
The book begins with “The Wall,” an account of a months-long road trip that follows stretches of the Great Wall.
All told, Hessler drives 7,436 miles across northern China, the once-great cultural heartland that nowadays is rapidly losing its population to massive urban migration.
Picking up hitchhikers, talking to locals, and visiting relics of the past, Hessler creates a vivid portrait of this changing region.
In “The Village,” he narrows his scope to a single settlement in the midst of transition.
North of Beijing, Hessler rents a farmhouse in a small, remote village called Sancha.
When Hessler first begins visiting Sancha, the place is accessible only by a dead-end dirt road, and it’s rare to see outsiders.
But soon the government undertakes a major road-building campaign across rural China, and the route to Sancha, like so many others, is paved.
Suddenly the village is accessible, and meanwhile the capital’s auto boom is creating a new class of citizens eager to explore the countryside.
In Sancha, a family called Wei tries to respond to these developments, shifting from farming to business.
Over the span of six years, Hessler observes in detail the effects of this change, which range from shifts in diet and health, to a growing interest in religion, to new tensions within the local branch of the Communist Party.
In “The Factory,” the third part of
*Country Driving*, Hessler turns his attention to urban China.
He spends three years researching in Lishui, a small southeastern city where officials hope that a new government-built expressway will transform a farm region into a major industrial center.
The city’s story is told through the experiences of one factory, which produces an obscure product: the nylon-coated metal rings that attach to bra straps.
For the typical American consumer, such an object doesn’t deserve a moment’s thought, but in China such it encompasses a world of determination and desire.
Fortunes have been made and lost, industrial espionage has been committed, even death threats have been issued — all in the service of producing this tiny, forgettable object.
Like so much of Hessler’s work, “The Factory” focuses on average people, telling the story of Lishui’s boom through the experiences of small-time entrepreneurs, migrant workers, and self-educated technicians.
And
*Country Driving* tracks the movement of the nation as a whole.
Having started in the countryside, with the stately image of the Great Wall, the book proceeds to new cities where migrants are engaged in producing objects like the bra ring — artifacts of the future.
Along the way, *Country Driving* illuminates the vast, shifting landscape of a traditionally rural nation that, having once built walls against outsiders, is now constructing factory towns to make goods for the world.
