## Summary

### Introduction

In the winter of 1937, the remote village of Okamura is gripped by both the excitement of a wedding and a creeping dread. Kenzo Ichiyanagi, the forty-year-old eldest son of a once-great family, is marrying Katsuko Kubo, a simple schoolteacher. To the Ichiyanagi family, proud descendants of a *honjin*-an inn for traveling nobility-this union is a scandalous drop in social status. Despite the family’s protests, the wedding proceeds in a small, private ceremony.

However, tragedy strikes the very next morning. Terrible screams and the frantic, wild music of a koto pierce the night. When the family finally gains access to the couple’s locked annex, they find a horrific scene. Kenzo and Katsuko have been slashed to death in a room locked from the inside. Outside, a bloody samurai sword stands upright in the garden, surrounded by pristine snow without a single footprint. It appears to be a perfect, impossible crime.

### The Impossible Crime

The local police are baffled by the locked-room puzzle. All entrances are sealed, and the snow surrounding the annex proves no one could have approached or left without leaving a trace. The only clues are the bloody fingerprints of a stranger inside the house and the ominous sword in the garden. The situation seems impossible, a narrative trap that the novel proudly acknowledges is a staple of the mystery genre.

Two days before the wedding, a suspicious man with a scarred face and only three fingers on his right hand was seen lurking around the village, asking for the Ichiyanagi house. On the wedding night, this stranger delivered a letter to Kenzo, which the groom tore up without reading. With the discovery of the three-fingered man’s fingerprints at the crime scene, the police believe they have their culprit. Yet, the locked room remains a paradox that no simple arrest can solve.

### Enter Detective Kosuke Kindaichi

Frustrated by the lack of progress, Ginzo Kubo, the bride’s uncle, summons Kosuke Kindaichi, an amateur detective. Kindaichi is an unlikely hero; he is young, scruffy, stammers, and looks more like a vagrant than a genius. However, he possesses a brilliant mind honed on a diet of Western detective novels.

Upon arrival, Kindaichi does not immediately scan for footprints or interrogate witnesses. Instead, he examines the bookshelves, noting the youngest brother Saburo’s extensive collection of crime fiction. This bibliographic detour proves crucial. Kindaichi realizes that the murderer is not only a killer but also an aficionado of the genre who has employed a complex mechanical trick to stage the impossible crime.

### The Mechanics of Murder

Kindaichi’s investigation reveals that the "Three-fingered Man" was a mere red herring. The fingerprint evidence was planted by the real killer to frame the outsider. Through patient questioning and the analysis of Kenzo’s meticulous diary, Kindaichi deduces the shocking truth: Kenzo Ichiyanagi took his own life and that of his wife.

The motive was rooted in Kenzo’s pathological obsession with cleanliness and honor. Upon discovering that his bride was not a virgin, Kenzo felt he could not dissolve the marriage without bringing shame upon his family lineage. His solution was to create a scenario that looked like a double murder, thereby preserving his honor post-mortem.

To achieve this, Kenzo utilized a complex mechanism. He attached the murder weapon, a sword, to a string connected to his koto. This string was fed through a gap in the traditional wall transom and tied to a water wheel outside. As the water wheel turned, it retracted the sword, pulling it from the room. The sword was then flung into the snow, creating the illusion that it had been thrown by an escaping murderer. Kenzo played the koto himself not out of artistry, but to mask the sound of the string sliding over the bamboo and the mechanism engaging.

### The Hidden Hand

While Kenzo’s suicide explains the deaths, it does not account for the full complexity of the plot. Kindaichi discovers that Kenzo’s younger brother, Saburo, was the true mastermind. Saburo, an avid reader of mysteries, caught his brother setting up the device. Rather than stopping him, Saburo decided to assist the plot to perfection, driven by a desire for the life insurance money and a darker, psychopathic urge to demonstrate his superiority.

Saburo’s role was insidious. He edited Kenzo’s diary to ensure the narrative of an intruder was convincing. He also assisted in obtaining fingerprints from the corpse of the "Three-fingered Man," an uninvolved bus driver who died of exhaustion nearby. Kenzo had cut off the man’s hand to use as a stamp for the fingerprints, while Saburo hid the severed hand in the grave of a family cat. The stranger who visited on the wedding night was actually Kenzo in disguise, orchestrating the final pieces of the frame-up.

### The Social Horror

While the locked-room mechanism provides intellectual stimulation, the novel saves its true horror for the motive. The Ichiyanagi family’s downfall is caused not by a mysterious villain, but by their own toxic adherence to tradition. Kenzo’s insecurity about his family’s declining status and his obsession with bloodline purity drive him to slaughter.

The anonymous narrator reflects on how the concept of lineage, while fading in the cities, still dominates life in rural Japan. The tragedy of the *Honjin Murders* is that the victims are sacrificed on the altar of this outdated social structure. By the end of the story, the family is destroyed-Saburo dies in the Second Sino-Japanese War, and other members pass away shortly after, marking the complete end of the Ichiyanagi wealth.

### Conclusion

*The Honjin Murders* stands as a bridge between Japanese literary tradition and the Western Golden Age of detective fiction. It uses the tropes of John Dickson Carr and Arthur Conan Doyle to tell a story that is deeply Japanese in its setting and social commentary. The resolution-a suicide disguised as murder involving a water wheel and a string instrument-may seem outlandish, but it serves as a stark metaphor for the contortions people will undergo to protect their reputations.

Readers are left with the image of the scruffy Kindaichi, who saw through the mechanical trickery to the human error at the heart of the crime. The novel reminds us that while puzzles can be solved with logic, the tragedies they often conceal are born of the inflexible human need to preserve an illusion of perfection.
