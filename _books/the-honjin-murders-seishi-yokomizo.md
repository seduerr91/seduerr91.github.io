## Hook

## Summary

### Introduction

In the winter of 1937, the remote village of Okamura is gripped by both the excitement of a wedding and a creeping dread. Kenzo Ichiyanagi, the forty-year-old eldest son of a once-great family, is marrying Katsuko Kubo, a simple schoolteacher. To the Ichiyanagi family, proud descendants of a *honjin*-an inn for traveling nobility-this union is a scandalous drop in social status. Despite the family’s protests, the wedding proceeds in a small, private ceremony.

However, tragedy strikes the very next morning. Terrible screams and the frantic, wild music of a koto pierce the night. When the family finally gains access to the couple’s locked annex, they find a horrific scene. Kenzo and Katsuko have been slashed to death in a room locked from the inside. Outside, a bloody samurai sword stands upright in the garden, surrounded by pristine snow without a single footprint. It appears to be a perfect, impossible crime.

### The Impossible Crime

The local police are baffled by the locked-room puzzle. All entrances are sealed, and the snow surrounding the annex proves no one could have approached or left without leaving a trace. The only clues are the bloody fingerprints of a stranger inside the house and the ominous sword in the garden. The situation seems impossible, a narrative trap that the novel proudly acknowledges is a staple of the mystery genre.

Two days before the wedding, a suspicious man with a scarred face and only three fingers on his right hand was seen lurking around the village, asking for the Ichiyanagi house. On the wedding night, this stranger delivered a letter to Kenzo, which the groom tore up without reading. With the discovery of the three-fingered man’s fingerprints at the crime scene, the police believe they have their culprit. Yet, the locked room remains a paradox that no simple arrest can solve.

### Enter Detective Kosuke Kindaichi

Frustrated by the lack of progress, Ginzo Kubo, the bride’s uncle, summons Kosuke Kindaichi, an amateur detective. Kindaichi is an unlikely hero; he is young, scruffy, stammers, and looks more like a vagrant than a genius. However, he possesses a brilliant mind honed on a diet of Western detective novels.

Upon arrival, Kindaichi does not immediately scan for footprints or interrogate witnesses. Instead, he examines the bookshelves, noting the youngest brother Saburo’s extensive collection of crime fiction. This bibliographic detour proves crucial. Kindaichi realizes that the murderer is not only a killer but also an aficionado of the genre who has employed a complex mechanical trick to stage the impossible crime.

### The Mechanics of Murder

Kindaichi’s investigation reveals that the "Three-fingered Man" was a mere red herring. The fingerprint evidence was planted by the real killer to frame the outsider. Through patient questioning and the analysis of Kenzo’s meticulous diary, Kindaichi deduces the shocking truth: Kenzo Ichiyanagi took his own life and that of his wife.

The motive was rooted in Kenzo’s pathological obsession with cleanliness and honor. Upon discovering that his bride was not a virgin, Kenzo felt he could not dissolve the marriage without bringing shame upon his family lineage. His solution was to create a scenario that looked like a double murder, thereby preserving his honor post-mortem.

To achieve this, Kenzo utilized a complex mechanism. He attached the murder weapon, a sword, to a string connected to his koto. This string was fed through a gap in the traditional wall transom and tied to a water wheel outside. As the water wheel turned, it retracted the sword, pulling it from the room. The sword was then flung into the snow, creating the illusion that it had been thrown by an escaping murderer. Kenzo played the koto himself not out of artistry, but to mask the sound of the string sliding over the bamboo and the mechanism engaging.

### The Hidden Hand

While Kenzo’s suicide explains the deaths, it does not account for the full complexity of the plot. Kindaichi discovers that Kenzo’s younger brother, Saburo, was the true mastermind. Saburo, an avid reader of mysteries, caught his brother setting up the device. Rather than stopping him, Saburo decided to assist the plot to perfection, driven by a desire for the life insurance money and a darker, psychopathic urge to demonstrate his superiority.

Saburo’s role was insidious. He edited Kenzo’s diary to ensure the narrative of an intruder was convincing. He also assisted in obtaining fingerprints from the corpse of the "Three-fingered Man," an uninvolved bus driver who died of exhaustion nearby. Kenzo had cut off the man’s hand to use as a stamp for the fingerprints, while Saburo hid the severed hand in the grave of a family cat. The stranger who visited on the wedding night was actually Kenzo in disguise, orchestrating the final pieces of the frame-up.

### The Social Horror

While the locked-room mechanism provides intellectual stimulation, the novel saves its true horror for the motive. The Ichiyanagi family’s downfall is caused not by a mysterious villain, but by their own toxic adherence to tradition. Kenzo’s insecurity about his family’s declining status and his obsession with bloodline purity drive him to slaughter.

The anonymous narrator reflects on how the concept of lineage, while fading in the cities, still dominates life in rural Japan. The tragedy of the *Honjin Murders* is that the victims are sacrificed on the altar of this outdated social structure. By the end of the story, the family is destroyed-Saburo dies in the Second Sino-Japanese War, and other members pass away shortly after, marking the complete end of the Ichiyanagi wealth.

### Conclusion

*The Honjin Murders* stands as a bridge between Japanese literary tradition and the Western Golden Age of detective fiction. It uses the tropes of John Dickson Carr and Arthur Conan Doyle to tell a story that is deeply Japanese in its setting and social commentary. The resolution-a suicide disguised as murder involving a water wheel and a string instrument-may seem outlandish, but it serves as a stark metaphor for the contortions people will undergo to protect their reputations.

Readers are left with the image of the scruffy Kindaichi, who saw through the mechanical trickery to the human error at the heart of the crime. The novel reminds us that while puzzles can be solved with logic, the tragedies they often conceal are born of the inflexible human need to preserve an illusion of perfection.

   Seishi Yokomizo’s* The Honjin Murders*, published in Japan in 1946 and now available in English for the first time, employs the plot tricks of early European and American mystery writers to tell the story of a rapidly changing Japanese society around the time of the Second World War.

The book’s narrator, who’s writing the novel years after the crime, is intrigued with the story in part because it seems to be a locked-room murder mystery, “a genre that any self-respecting detective novelist will attempt at some point in his or her career.”
Instead of giving the reader the facts in the case up front, the narrator at first gushes about all the other existing mystery plots that might be similar to this one, name dropping the works of American John Dickson Carr and French Maurice Leblanc.
*The Honjin Murders* can be satisfyingly read as a classic who-done-it, although through the lens of one of Japan’s most famous writers of the genre.
The book allows Yokomizo, who died in 1981 after a prolific career, to introduce readers to his famous detective, Kosuke Kindaichi, for the first time.

It’s a wonder this short novel, clocking in at about 180 pages, took so long to be published in English.
The way Yokomizo chooses to launch his most famous creation into the fictional world is a nod to all those outcast detectives of Western literature that came before him.
When Kindaichi arrives on the scene about midway through the book, he doesn’t look promising.

The Ichiyanagi family proudly used to own a *honjin*, allowing them valuable status during the era.

The Ichiyanagis surrendered their *honjin*, but through the decades that followed, they held onto the status that came with it.
Lineage is important: once a descendant of a *honjin*, always a descendant of a *honjin*.

Inheritances, cultural or monetary, can motivate people to do bad things.
This story being a locked-room mystery, the narrator spends almost as much time describing the inanimate as the animate.
There are detailed tours of the Ichiyanagi compound, its surroundings and its particular architecture.

   **The Honjin Murders [1973/2019] – ★★★★1/2**
...
*The Honjin Murders* is considered to be the classic Japanese murder mystery, first serialised in 1946 and published in 1973.
It is a debut work of the author and the winner of the first Mystery Writers of Japan Award.
This story centres on the well-to-do Ichiyanagi family living in the village of Okamura who prepare for the wedding of their eldest son– Kenzo to a young woman of humbler origins – Katsuko.
The whole village is shaken when both Kenzo and Katsuko are found slashed to death in their room in the early morning hours after their wedding.
One strange clue follows another and soon it becomes clear that the murderer could not have possibly escaped the premises after the commission of the murder.
The local police feels stuck with this case, and it is at this point that one young and unassuming amateur detective Kosuke Kindaichi takes his turn to try to solve this highly unusual “locked-room” mystery.
Offering a curious insight into traditional Japan, *The Honjin Murders* is a compact, tightly-woven crime mystery, which, while paying a direct tribute to other crime masterworks, provides its own similar brain-teaser.
The book is narrated by a person who is unconnected to the murders and who simply tells us about one intriguing murder case he has heard about – the murder that happened within the clan of Ichiyanagi, a family who has always prided themselves on their lineage, being the direct descendants of those who kept a *honjin* (an inn where nobility would stay on the way to the Emperor) in the Edo Period.
The distinguishing feature of the book is that the narrator first quite painstakingly introduces us to the setting, to all the family members within the household and to the unusual circumstances of the murder.
How could this murder have possibly been committed if the murderer hardly had any means of escaping after he or she committed their murder?; what could be the significance of the *koto*, a Japanese stringed musical instrument, being played shortly after the murder?; and why the* katana* that was used to kill the couple was found in the snow some metres away form the house?
Moreover, two days before the murder, witnesses reported seeing a suspicious-looking old man loitering near the Ichiyanagi estate while also asking directions to the main house.
The police is dumbfounded by the case, especially when they learn that Kenzo, the victim of the murder, also received a mysterious note shortly before his wedding.
Could someone from Kenzo’s close family (such as his mother Itoko, his two younger brothers Ryuji and Saburo, or his cousin Ryosuke), who all initially opposed his wedding, have been involved in the gruesome murders?
*The Honjin Murders* is a kind of a mystery which is an instant page-turner that keeps one guessing until the very end.
The vivid images that it evokes, such as the snow, “*a red-ochre-painted room and the sound of the koto….*” [Yokomizo/Heal Kawai, 1973/2019: 11] are memorable, even if the characters remain somewhat distant, too many and not explored.
There is something in *The Honjin Muders* from Arthur Conan Doyle’s *Sherlock Holmes* series (a detective that uses hard logic to solve cases), from Agatha Christie’s *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd * [1926] (an unassuming detective and similar plot points) and from Gaston Leroux *The Mystery of the Yellow Room* [1907] (one seemingly “impossible” crime and similar plot points).
And, it seems that* The Honjin Murders* does not shy away from these comparisons, but welcomes and pretty much emphasises them.
In that way, the book pays a very direct tribute to all of its predecessors and masters of this tricky genre, trying to bring different pieces of them together, while also contributing with its own unusual addition.
Surprisingly, although I did not like Ayatsuji’s *The Decagon House Murders * [1987], I did find that many of the similar elements from that book worked well in *The Honjin Murders*, including the desire to imitate other world-famous crime mysteries and the focus on plot logistics over characters.
Perhaps, Yokomizo’s unpretentious and even endearing style of writing helps to win the readers over, and it is very likely that the explanations behind the final solution will prove to be more believable for the Japanese than for the Western readers.
*The Honjin Murders* is not exactly literary and may be too crowded with various characters for such a short book.
However, the mystery is also a very carefully set out one with plenty of red herrings and surprises along the way.
This book, which evokes so vividly all the “attributes” of the unique murder – from the Japanese *koto* to the *katana* lodged in the snow, is a highly enjoyable debut.

A newly married couple are killed on their wedding night in the annexe of a family mansion.
A Japanese sword is found embedded in the ground outside, surrounded by snow but no footprints.
Inside the bedroom there is a lot of blood plus bloody fingerprints.
