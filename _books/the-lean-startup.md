   ***The Lean Startup: How Today's Entrepreneurs Use Continuous Innovation to Create Radically Successful Businesses** * is a 2011 book by American entrepreneur Eric Ries.
It outlines the lean startup methodology, a framework for startup development that prioritizes rapid prototyping, validated learning, and iterative product releases.
The goal of this methodology is to shorten product development cycles.
The methodology advocates for building a minimum viable product and gathering user feedback to refine the offering of a product over time.
Ries reports developing this approach based on his experiences as a startup advisor and founder, following challenges faced with his first startup.
The lean startup method draws on ideas from lean manufacturing and agile development.
It focuses on adapting strategies based on experimentation and user feedback rather than relying on long-term business planning.

## The Lean Startup Methodology
The core of Ries’ approach is the lean startup methodology.
This methodology is based on the idea that startups should focus on creating a minimum viable product (MVP) **as quickly as possible, and then iterate and improve based on feedback from customers**.
The lean startup methodology is designed to help startups avoid the pitfalls of traditional product development.
Instead of spending months or even years building a product that may not be successful, the methodology encourages entrepreneurs to test their assumptions early and often.
...
Next, one of the key ideas in *The Lean Startup* is the concept of **validated learning**.
Validated learning suggests that entrepreneurs focus on **learning as much as possible about their customers and their needs**.
This can be done through customer interviews, surveys, and other forms of feedback.
The goal of validated learning is to reduce uncertainty and risk.
By testing assumptions and gathering feedback, entrepreneurs can make better decisions about how to improve their products and grow their businesses.
Ries also discusses two fundamental hypotheses to be evaluated for a new product:
- The *value hypothesis* tests whether a product or service really delivers value to customers once they are using it.
Experiments provide a more accurate gauge.
- The *growth hypothesis* tests how new customers will discover a product or service.
...
The Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop is a central component of the lean startup methodology.
This loop involves building a minimum viable product, measuring its performance, and then learning from the results.
The key to the Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop is **speed**.
Entrepreneurs need to move quickly to test their assumptions and gather feedback.
This means building a minimum viable product that can be tested quickly, and then iterating based on feedback.
...
Innovation Accounting is a concept that is closely related to the Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop.
It is the process of **measuring progress** in a startup by using metrics that are based on validated learning.
Innovation Accounting is important because it helps entrepreneurs measure their progress in a meaningful way.
By tracking metrics that are based on validated learning, you can make better decisions about how to grow your business.
Eric Ries suggests the following three steps:
1. Use a minimum viable product to establish real data on where the company is right now — this gives a clear picture of the current status
2. Attempt to tune the “engine” from baseline toward the ideal — this may take many attempts
3. Reach a decision point after making all the micro changes and product optimization — the “pivot or persevere” decision
Entrepreneurs should also be careful to not rely on **vanity metrics**.
Vanity metrics can allow entrepreneurs to form false conclusions and live their own private realities.
For instance, Ries provides some examples:
- Instead of looking at gross metrics, switch to *cohort-based metrics*
- Instead of looking at cause-and-effect relationships, switch to *split-test (A/B) experiments*
...
This is a critical moment.
Pivoting is another key concept discussed in *The Lean Startup*.
A pivot is a change in direction based on feedback collected.
When entrepreneurs discover that their assumptions are incorrect, they need to be willing to change direction and try something new.
**It is a tough decision.**
Pivoting is not easy, but it is essential for success in the world of entrepreneurship.
By being open to change and willing to pivot when necessary, entrepreneurs can increase their chances of success.
Ries provides a dozen of examples of different kinds of pivots.
Here are the ones I found most interesting:
- A *zoom-in pivot* is when a feature becomes the whole product
- A *zoom-out pivot,* on the other hand, is when the whole product becomes a feature
- A *customer segment pivot* is when a different customer is now targeted, different from the originally anticipated
- An *engine of growth pivot* is when a company switches one of the three engines of growth
Ries also discusses three **engines of growth** that cover most if not all the product scenarios:
1. The *sticky* engine of growth: the product grows based on stickiness with users; as long as the rate of new customer acquisition is higher than the churn rate, the product will grow.
2. The *viral* engine of growth: the product grows based on network effects.
As long as users recommend the product, the product will grow.
3. The *paid* engine of growth: the product grows based on sales and marketing efforts.
As long as the company invests in sales and marketing, the product will grow.
...

I found the methodology to be a robust framework for building successful products in today’s rapidly changing world.
By leveraging the Build-Measure-Learn loop, focusing on validated learning, and using innovation accounting, entrepreneurs and companies alike can increase their chances of success.
The book becomes a practical guide for anyone building products, and something I will likely re-read in a few years to refresh my memory!

### How The Book is Organized
- #### Vision
**Vision** makes the case for a new discipline of entrepreneurial management.
Identify who is an entrepreneur, define a startup, and articulate a new way for startups to gauge if they are making progress, called validated learning.
To achieve that learning, we'll see that startups - in a garage or inside an enterprise - can use scientific experimentation to discover how to build a sustainable business.
- #### Steer
**Steer** dives into the Lean Startup method in detail, showing one major turn through the core Build- Measure- Learn feedback loop.
Beginning with leap-of-faith assumptions that cry out for vigorous testing, you'll learn how to build a minimum viable product to test those assumptions, a new accounting system for evaluating whether you're making progress, and a method for deciding whether to pivot (changing course with one foot anchored to the ground) or persevere.
- #### Accelerate
In **Accelerate**, we'll explore techniques that enable Lean Startups to speed through the Build- Measure- Learn feedback loop as quickly as possible, even as they scale.
We'll explore lean manufacturing concepts that are applicable to startups, too, such as the power of small batches.
We'll also discuss organizational design, how products grow, and how to apply Lean Startup principles beyond the proverbial garage, even inside the world's largest companies.

### About the Author Eric Ries
Eric is the author of the popular blog Startup Lessons Learned and the creator of the Lean Startup methodology.
He co-founded and served as CTO of IMVU, his third startup, which has today has over 40 million users and 2009 revenue over $22 million.
An entrepreneur in residence at Harvard Business School and a frequent speaker at business events, he advises startups on business and product strategy using the Lean Startup approach.
