## Summary

### From Mentor to Manager: A Tech Leader's Roadmap

The transition from writing code to leading people is rarely straightforward. In an industry often lacking structured resources for leadership development, technical professionals frequently find themselves navigating these complex waters without a map. Camille Fournier’s *The Manager’s Path* acts as that essential guide, offering a mentor’s perspective on the evolution from a skilled individual contributor to a senior executive. It bridges the critical gap between technical prowess and the soft skills required for effective management, providing a chronological framework for the challenges faced at every stage of a tech leadership career.

#### The Foundation: Mentoring and Tech Leads

The journey often begins not with a promotion, but with a subtle shift in responsibility toward mentorship. As an experienced engineer, you are expected to guide junior colleagues, helping them identify their potential and navigate their early careers. This stage is about exercising influence without necessarily possessing managerial authority. Success here requires listening-a precursor to empathy-and creating a safe environment where mentees feel comfortable sharing their struggles.

As these responsibilities grow, one often steps into the role of Tech Lead. This role represents a critical mindset shift: your productivity matters less than the team’s productivity. Tech leads are not simply the best coders on the team; they are project managers who must balance technical contributions with leadership duties. The primary error new leads make is confusing leadership with authority or swinging too far into management and neglecting the building process entirely.

To succeed, a tech lead must focus on clearing roadblocks and unblocking the team. This involves mastering project management skills, such as breaking down large deliverables into smaller, manageable tasks and pushing through unknowns. The lead serves as a bridge between the team and management, translating technical intricacies and representing the group's interests. Staying technically relevant remains a core tenet, but the goal shifts to improving the system of work that developers operate within.

#### Managing People and Conflict

Moving from managing projects to managing people requires a deep investment in relationships. Employees should expect their managers to invest in their growth through three key vehicles: regularly scheduled one-on-one meetings, continuous feedback, and resources for career development. These meetings are not status updates but opportunities to build human connection and discuss work-related issues privately.

A significant hurdle at this stage is conflict resolution. Great managers give corrective feedback immediately rather than delaying difficult conversations, thereby shortening feedback loops. When conflict arises, avoid relying solely on consensus or voting. Instead, make decisions depersonalized by relying on a set of objective standards and deferring to the team member with the highest expertise in the specific area. Fournier warns against turning a blind eye to simmering issues; addressing problems early prevents them from becoming toxic.

Micromanagement is another trap new managers face, often stemming from a desire for control. Rather than dominating every decision, managers should ask their team how they are measuring success. If the team isn’t measuring it, coaching is required. If they are, ask them to make it visible. This approach empowers the team and builds trust without the manager needing to hover.

#### Scaling: Teams and Strategy

As scope expands to managing multiple teams, the challenges shift from execution to delegation and prioritization. Managers must learn to let go of tasks, even those they find easy. Delegation is essential for career growth-both the manager’s and the team’s. Tasks should be categorized; simple, frequent tasks like minor code reviews can be delegated, while simple, infrequent tasks may be handled personally. Leaders must also learn to say no effectively, using strategies like "Yes, and..." to explain the impact of a new request or "Not right now" to reprioritize work.

At the senior level, such as a CTO or VP of Engineering, the role becomes about strategic direction and protecting the organization. A key responsibility is preventing engineering from becoming a mere execution arm. By driving education that exposes engineers to business and customer outcomes, leaders help the team see opportunities that non-engineers might miss. This requires constant communication; a good rule of thumb is that a message must be communicated about seven times before it is truly understood.

#### Culture and Structure

Leadership also involves shaping the structural and cultural environment of the organization. Fournier advocates for the creation of clear career ladders or growth frameworks. Without these, performance assessments become highly subjective and susceptible to bias. A good ladder should split management and technical tracks, ensuring that those with strong technical skills can advance without being forced into people management roles. It should also involve the team in its creation and consider "breakpoint" levels where employees must either advance or move on.

Cultivating culture is not passive; it requires defining core values and leading by example. Culture is shaped by the behaviors leaders reward and the expectations they set. When hiring, for instance, it is crucial to assess candidates against these values rather than just their technical output. Furthermore, Fournier advises that you must be able to manage yourself before you can effectively manage others. This requires self-awareness, emotional regulation, and the ability to work through conflict constructively.

#### Taking Ownership of the Path

Ultimately, *The Manager’s Path* emphasizes that navigating one’s career is a personal responsibility. Whether you are an individual contributor or a senior leader, you must define what you want and communicate those goals clearly. If your manager is neglectful or difficult, you must advocate for yourself, ask for what you need, and if necessary, choose your manager wisely in future roles. By combining strategic oversight with a commitment to personal growth and team empowerment, tech leaders can successfully navigate the complexities of the industry and build enduring, high-performing teams.

## Being a tech lead is about growing and exercising influence
Tech lead is usually a role and set of responsibilities, not a title.
To fulfill those responsibilities a tech lead needs to grow their sphere of influence and then exercise that influence for the good of the organization.
The first place new tech leads go wrong is not differentiating managerial authority from leadership and influence.
They wrongly assume that successful leadership requires managerial authority.
The second place where tech leads go wrong is perceiving themselves as managers or pseudo-managers.
They start thinking of themselves and their calendar quickly fills with managerial tasks.
They swing too far and the team stalls.
These tech leads need to get back to the work of building.
## Tech leads are primarily developing project management skills
The path from engineer to people manager runs through project management.
If you’re wondering how you get into people management, it usually happens not because you’re a friendly person but rather because you have consistently demonstrated your ability to lead projects to success.
Project management skills make or break a tech lead.
Engineering managers expect that tech leads can reliably deliver projects.
Your reward for successful delivery is another project of higher complexity.
## As tech lead your productivity matters less than team productivity
On the path from individual contributor to manager, the tech lead stage represents a critical point shift in mindset from self to team productivity.
You need to start paying closer attention to how people on your team are producing, volunteering to help them work through tough problems, developing tooling and systems to improve everyone’s productivity, and communicating on behalf of the team.
## Managers fail when they avoid conflict and do not provide corrective feedback immediately
Any manager can give positive feedback.
Good managers give constructive feedback periodically.
Great managers give corrective feedback immediately.
A more general principle here is to always reduce feedback loops.
In development that could mean faster automated tests.
In people management that can mean providing immediate feedback, particularly when that feedback is corrective because corrective feedback is the most difficult to provide and most tempting to avoid.
## When managers feel the urge to micromanage, they should ask their team how success is being measured and to make it visible
Micromanaging comes down to our desire for control among perceived chaos.
Rather than giving into our controlling urges and trying to dominate the conversation, we as managers should first ask our teams how they are measuring success.
If they aren’t measuring success, then you have some coaching to do.
If they are measuring success, ask them to make it visible so you can see progress without interrupting them.
## Managing engineers is easier and more effective when they respect your technical skills and you have the ability to assess quality
Engineering managers need to establish and maintain high standards, which requires assessing work quality from their employees.
Assessing quality is much easier for technical engineering managers for two reasons.
First, you have the skills to assess quality.
You know what quality code looks like.
You also know what sloppy code looks like.
Second, when your direct reports know that you are assessing quality and that you have the technical skills to do so, they pay more attention to the details.
Engineering managers having technical skills supports a virtuous cycle of quality.
## Watch deployment frequency closely to understand team productivity and satisfaction
Deployment frequency is one of the four key metrics of successful software delivery performance.
So we know about using deployment frequency as a measure of productivity.
But productivity gains aren’t the only goal.
Productive teams are usually happy teams.
Engineers want to feel productive.
## The CTO must protect engineering from being a pure execution arm
To the extent that engineering teams are simply production and execution arms of product development, they are replaceable and non-value add.
When you train engineers to think from a customer outcomes perspective, they see opportunities that non-engineers can’t see by virtue of their position.
A good CTO will drive education and training for their engineers, exposing them to business and customer outcomes and goals, which prevents engineering from being a pure execution arm.
## Never underestimate how many times you need to communicate something
A good rule of thumb is you need to communicate something about seven times before people start to really understand it, likely across several mediums to ensure you’re hitting both readers and listeners.

In the book The Manager’s Path Camille Fournier explores managing engineers and what it takes to be a technical manager.
She describes the different roles which form the path from mentors and tech leads to senior engineering management, discusses the challenges of technical leadership and provides advice on how to deal with them.

InfoQ interviewed Fournier about what employees expect from their manager, where things often go wrong and how to deal with that, challenges for technical professionals when they are promoted to become tech leads, what tech leads can do to create a culture of continuous feedback, how to balance technical and management tasks, and what it takes for tech leads to become a top level manager.

> **Camille Fournier**: When I wrote the first draft of the book, I had just finished an intense period of learning how to manage and lead an engineering organization, and all of these hard lessons were fresh on my mind.
I didn’t think there was a lot out there about the path that many people take as engineering managers, from mentor all the way through to senior management.
...
**Fournier**: This book is intended for anyone who is interested in engineering management.
For those who are early in their career and curious about what that path might entail, the book provides an idea of some of the opportunities and challenges that might present themselves if they choose to follow this path.
For those in management roles, my hope is that this provides some pragmatic and focused advice about common challenges that engineering managers will experience.

**InfoQ: What are the main things that employees expect from their manager?**
>
**Fournier**: The biggest things employees should expect from their manager are regularly-scheduled 1-1 meetings, feedback, and resources for career growth.
Your manager should be able to guide you through the ins and outs of your workplace, in order to help you get around roadblocks in your work, and they should know what resources are available for you to get training.
Some managers will act as coaches for their employees, but this is not always something that your manager can provide.
Managers might provide technical feedback, and sometimes set the roadmap for projects for the team, but it’s not universally true that every manager you have will hold these responsibilities.
...
And unfortunately, some managers are cruel to their employees, manipulating, micromanaging, or even bully them.
...
>
**Fournier**: The #1 thing you can do is, as much as you can, choose your manager wisely.
We don’t always have the power to choose our managers, but identifying people you want to work for in your workplace is a good idea.
When you’re interviewing for a job, ask about the manager you would be reporting to and look for managers who seem to have a good reputation with the people who work for them.
...
> I also advise people to remember that ultimately, we’re all adults and responsible for our own destiny.
That means that you should spend some time thinking about what you want for yourself, and what you want for your career.
When you know what you want, you can go out and find it more easily, and you can ask your manager for what you want if you aren’t getting it.
If they keep canceling your 1-1s, tell them that you would like to get on a more regular schedule.
Ask them for feedback explicitly.
...
> If your manager turns out to be cruel or harmful, take care of yourself.
...
>
**Fournier**: First, recognize that while you are the tech lead, that doesn’t make you the technical dictator of the team.
Figure out which technical decisions should be made by you, which should be handed off to other team members who have more expertise, and which decisions should involve the whole team coming to a consensus.
> Second, be careful of getting too obsessed with processes to solve problems that are related to communication or leadership gaps.
Some tech leads think that the solution to every problem is a new or better process.
...
> Third, don’t step entirely away from the code.
As a tech lead, you should be writing code some of the time.
It may only be 30% of your time, but that is enough for you to be staying in the mix of what it is like to write software on your team.
For people who become engineering managers, staying technical is an important element to management success.
...
>
**Fournier**: Spend time getting to know your new team as individuals.
If you are coming in to manage a team of individual contributors, don’t just focus on understanding the projects and the tech.
