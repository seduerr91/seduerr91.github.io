## Summary

##### The Parallel Paths of War
Nina Willner’s *The Boys in the Light* is an immersive work of narrative nonfiction that reconstructs the intersecting lives of three young men caught in the gears of World War II. The story begins by tracing the separate trajectories of Eddie Willner, a Jewish teenager from Germany, and two Americans, Elmer Hovland and Sammy DeCola.

Eddie grew up in a middle-class family in Cologne, where his father was a decorated World War I veteran. As the Nazi regime tightened its grip, Eddie’s family initially believed they could weather the political storm. However, the escalating violence following Kristallnacht forced them to flee to France. Meanwhile, in rural Minnesota and urban Massachusetts, Elmer and Sammy were living typical American lives. When the United States entered the war, these boys-only slightly older than Eddie-enlisted in the Army. They were eventually assigned to D Company of the 32nd Armored Regiment, part of the 3rd Armored Division, unaware that their path was destined to cross with Eddie’s in the heart of Germany.

##### The Descent into Darkness
The Willner family’s search for safety ended in tragedy when they were betrayed in France. Arrested and transported to the Drancy internment camp, they were eventually deported to Auschwitz. Upon arrival, the Nazis immediately sent Eddie’s mother to the gas chambers. Eddie and his father were selected for slave labor, stripped of their identities, and tattooed.

They were transferred to subcamps like Blechhammer, where conditions were brutal. At Blechhammer, prisoners were forced to work on synthetic fuel production, a site so critical it became a top target for Allied bombings. While the raids brought danger, they also offered a strange comfort to the prisoners, proving they had not been forgotten by the outside world. During these chaotic raids, Eddie and his friend Maurits “Mike” Swaab, a Dutch orphan he met at Auschwitz, began small acts of sabotage, severing wires and destroying equipment to undermine the German war effort.

##### The Grind of Endurance
The narrative moves from Blechhammer to the Langenstein-Zwieberge subcamp of Buchenwald, where conditions were so harsh that the average prisoner survived only six weeks. The work involved excavating rock to build tunnels for aircraft factories. It was here that Eddie’s father, weakened by starvation and illness, was murdered by the SS.

Before his death, Eddie’s father had instilled in him a strict discipline and made him memorize the names of two dozen family members. This training became a lifeline. The book emphasizes that survival in the camps was not the result of a single heroic escape but rather the accumulation of thousands of daily decisions. The boys mastered the art of evasion, learning to read patrols and move silently. They bartered small skills for food and navigated the social hierarchy of the prisoners. Survival was a constant, grinding negotiation with fear.

##### Faith and Friendship
Amidst the dehumanization, faith and brotherhood emerged as critical survival mechanisms. The text describes faith as a “portable homeland”-a set of rituals and whispered prayers that allowed the boys to remember who they were when the world tried to erase them. This spiritual discipline provided a moral compass and a reason to endure.

Equally important was the bond between Eddie and Mike. They formed a miniature society with its own rules and shared resources. When one faltered, the other carried the load. This mutual aid was a strategic choice as much as an emotional one. By pooling their strength, they transformed from vulnerable individuals into a resilient unit capable of absorbing the shocks of camp life. Their commitment to one another was fueled by the grief of losing their families and a determination to keep the light of humanity alive.

##### The Escape and the Rescue
As the Allies closed in, the SS forced the prisoners on a death march. During this chaos, Eddie and Mike seized the opportunity to escape. After five days of wandering through the German countryside, starving and exposed, they followed the sounds of artillery.

They stumbled upon elements of D Company, 32nd Armored Regiment. Led by 23-year-old Elmer Hovland and including cook Sammy “Pepsi” DeCola, the American soldiers were battle-hardened and weary. They had fought through Normandy and the Siegfried Line, questioning the purpose of the carnage they witnessed. That changed when they saw two “withered skeletons” emerge from the woods, tattooed arms raised in surrender. Weighing only 75 pounds, Eddie was the living embodiment of the Nazi regime’s evil.

For the American GIs, rescuing these boys brought a sudden, profound clarity to their mission. The unit “adopted” Eddie and Mike, nursing them back to health over several months. The teenagers used their knowledge of German positions to assist the advancing American tanks, completing a circle of rescue and reciprocity.

##### A Legacy of Brotherhood
The impact of this encounter rippled through decades. After the war, Eddie learned that 26 members of his family had been murdered. He and Mike eventually emigrated to the United States, where they built new lives. Eddie served in the U.S. Army for over two decades, while Mike served in the Air Force. Similarly, Elmer and Sammy returned home to start families and businesses.

The soldiers of D Company held reunions for nearly fifty years, always wondering what had become of the two boys they saved. For decades, Eddie’s family searched for the soldiers, fearing they were lost to history. In a remarkable conclusion, the author describes a reunion almost six decades after the war. The survivors and their rescuers were able to close the circle, finding peace in knowing that the bonds forged in the darkness of the forests of Germany had resulted in lives well lived.

*The Boys in the Light* is ultimately a story of how goodness can triumph over evil. It honors the resilience of the human spirit, the necessity of moral courage, and the enduring power of the brotherhood that kept the light alive during the world’s darkest hour.
