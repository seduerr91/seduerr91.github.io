## Hook

An average journalist trained for just one year and won the U.S. Memory Championship, proving that anyone can master the lost art of remembering.

## Overview

Moonwalking with Einstein is a participatory journalism deep-dive into the world of competitive memorization. Joshua Foer investigates the techniques used by 'mental athletes,' tracing their lineage back to ancient times, and discovers that these methods rely on exploiting the brain's natural preference for spatial and visual information. The book dismantles the myth that memory capacity is fixed, introducing readers to strategies like the memory palace, the Major System, and the Person-Action-Object (PAO) system. It also emphasizes the science of expertise, explaining how chunking and deliberate practice allow individuals to perform seemingly impossible mental feats.

## Summary

Moonwalking with Einstein explores the art and science of remembering everything, documenting Joshua Foer's journey from a forgetful journalist to the 2006 U.S. Memory Champion. The book argues that exceptional memory is not a genetic talent but a learned skill. Foer traces the history of memory from ancient oral traditions to the age of the internet, explaining how the invention of the printing press and smartphones diminished the cultural value of internal memory. However, neuroscience reveals that memory champions do not have different brain structures than average people; they simply use specific techniques to encode data more effectively. Central to the book is the "memory palace" (or method of loci), a technique that leverages the brain's powerful spatial memory to store abstract information as vivid images. Foer also introduces concepts like chunking, which allows experts to organize information into larger patterns, and the "OK plateau," a stage where learning stops unless deliberate practice is applied. Ultimately, the book suggests that training our memory is essential not just for facts, but for enriching our experience of time and life.

## Key ideas

### Memory has declined historically

Summary: Historically, internal memory was vital for survival and culture, but the invention of the printing press and later the internet allowed us to externalize information, causing our collective memory skills to atrophy.

Why it matters: We often blame our biology for poor memory, but it is largely a cultural shift away from the need to retain information internally.

Try this: Audit how often you rely on your phone for basic facts; try to recall a piece of information without checking an external device first.

### Memory is a skill, not a talent

Summary: Declarative memories (facts and events) rely on the hippocampus, while non-declarative memories (skills like riding a bike) do not. Brain scans of memory champions reveal no structural differences from average people, confirming that superior memory is a result of technique, not biology.

Why it matters: Understanding that memory is a learned capability removes the mental barrier that you are simply 'bad at remembering.'

Try this: Adopt the mindset that your memory is malleable; approach memorization tasks as practice sessions rather than tests of innate intelligence.

### Use the Memory Palace technique

Summary: The memory palace involves placing vivid mental images of what you want to remember along a familiar spatial route. This exploits the brain's evolutionary strength for spatial memory and navigation.

Why it matters: It transforms abstract data into spatial data, which the human brain is evolutionarily hardwired to retain much more effectively.

Try this: Visualize your childhood home and mentally place a gallon of milk exploding on your kitchen table to help you remember to buy milk.

### Employ elaborate encoding

Summary: Elaborative encoding is the process of translating boring information into something the brain excels at remembering-specifically, visual, spatial, and emotional narratives. The 'Baker/baker paradox' illustrates that we remember a profession (baker) better than a name (Baker) because the profession carries imagery and associations.

Why it matters: Creating strong, multi-sensory connections provides the necessary 'cues' the brain needs to retrieve stored information later.

Try this: When meeting a new person, create a bizarre visual association linking their name to a distinct feature of their face or profession.

### Master the art of chunking

Summary: Chunking is the process of breaking individual bits of information into larger, meaningful blocks. Experts in fields like chess or memorization use this to bypass the limits of short-term memory (usually 7 items) by seeing patterns rather than isolated details.

Why it matters: It expands the capacity of working memory, allowing you to process and recall much more information than the standard limit allows.

Try this: Group numbers in a long sequence into dates, familiar area codes, or significant years rather than trying to memorize individual digits.

### Break through the OK Plateau

Summary: The 'OK plateau' is the stage where a skill becomes automatic and improvement stops. To continue advancing, one must engage in deliberate practice-consciously focusing on weaknesses and pushing beyond comfort zones.

Why it matters: Without the discomfort of deliberate practice, skills plateau at 'good enough,' preventing the transition from competence to mastery.

Try this: If you are learning a language or an instrument, force yourself to attempt speeds or complexity slightly above your current ability rather than repeating what you already know well.

## Action steps

1. Build a memory palace by selecting a familiar place, like your home, and mentally placing vivid, bizarre images of items you want to remember at specific locations along a route.
2. Practice chunking by breaking long strings of data-such as phone numbers or dates-into smaller, meaningful groups to extend your short-term memory capacity.
3. Apply elaborate encoding by turning abstract information into colorful, multi-sensory, and emotionally charged mental images to make them stickier.
4. Escape the 'OK plateau' by pushing past automaticity; practice specifically at the edge of your ability and focus on your weaknesses rather than relying on repetition alone.

- Make improvements over time

Joshua Foer forgot things like the rest of us until by chance he came across the world of mental athletes.
*Moonwalking with Einstein* documents Foer’s journey from basic memory to elite memory, culminating with victory at the US Memory Championship within a year.
The book takes an extensive tour through the science and history of memory, explaining how anyone can practice the memory techniques of mental athletes – and perhaps more importantly, why the art of memory matters.

Beginning in documented history with Simonides, historically the art of memory was considered a vital instrument for innovation.
But as Foer notes, over millennia we have gradually supplanted our own natural memory with a vast structure of external memory aids – a process dramatically sped up by the internet and digitalisation.
...
Concepts like Mind Mapping – a simple diagrammatical technique for committing a large range of ideas to memory – have sought to revive our use of internal memory.
But the truth is that further digitalisation will inevitably leave us in an increased architecture of external memory.
So why does that matter?
Foer points to the anecdotal link between memory and creativity, but more importantly, how memory forms our interactions with the world.
As Foer puts it,
*“How we perceive the world and how we act in it are products of how and what we remember.”* **Key Idea #2: The science of memory systems**
Scientists generally divide memory systems into two types:
**Declarative memories:**things you know you remember, e.g. what happened yesterday.
**Non-declarative memories:**things you know unconsciously, e.g. how to ride a bike.
Studies of those who have lost their hippocampi tell us that non-declarative memories don’t need the same short-term memory buffer as declarative memories.
What’s more, they don’t depend on the hippocampus to be consolidated and stored.
Instead, these memories rely on other parts of the brain, such as the cerebellum for motor skill learning, neocortex for perceptual learning, and basal ganglia for habit learning.
Declarative memories are distinguished into two further categories:
**Semantic memories:**facts and concepts **Episodic memories:**experiences of our own lives
These two types of memory use different brain regions and rely on different neural pathways.

Memories typically erode over the so-called “curve of forgetting”.
But studies show that images tend to be much more resistant over time.
For example, tests have exposed participants to thousands of images and then asked them to choose from two images (one of the thousands they had seen and one new image).
Participants consistently recall a significant proportion, and this effect appears to be robust even after longer time periods have elapsed.
The idea of a permanent photographic memory, however, is just a myth.
In reality, memories mutate, deplete, and eventually disappear.
But our particular talent for images offers hints at the most effective techniques for remembering things.
It seems that the key to memory is to organise memories into images along a familiar journey.
Neurological research lends support to this idea.
Studies have shown that when memory champions engage in memorisation activities, they display higher activity in the brain regions associated with special memory and navigation.

**Person-action-object (PAO):**This is a key technique for longer number sequences.
Every two-digit number from 00 to 99 represents a person performing an action on an object, e.g. David Beckham kicking a football.
Any six-digit number can then be turned into a single image by combining the person from the first two digits, the action from the third and fourth digit, and the object from the last two digits.
(This same technique is what most memory competitors use from remembering decks of cards.)

The core technique of the book—and the primary weapon of every serious memory
competitor.
The memory palace (also called the method of loci) works by converting
abstract information into vivid mental images and placing those images along a familiar
route through a physical space you know well.
Here's how it works: think of your house.
...
The more bizarre, vivid, and emotionally charged the images, the better they stick.

It's been independently discovered by multiple cultures because it exploits a fundamental feature of human cognition: **our spatial

## Key Idea 2: Elaborative Encoding
The memory palace works because of a deeper principle: **elaborative
encoding**.
This is the process of converting boring, abstract information into vivid, multi-sensory, emotionally charged mental images.
The more elaborate the encoding, the more retrieval cues your brain creates, and the easier the memory is to find later.
Foer describes the PAO (Person-Action-Object) system that memory competitors use for

Every two-digit number from 00 to 99 gets assigned a person, an action, and an
object.
...
The point is that it

brains evolved to handle: spatial, visual, emotional, and narrative**.

Completely different recall.

Foer introduces the concept of the "OK plateau"—the point where you stop improving at
