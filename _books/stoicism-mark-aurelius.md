## Hook

What if the secret to inner peace was written by a Roman Emperor solely for himself, never meant to be published?

## Overview

Based on the private journals of Marcus Aurelius, this briefing explores Stoic philosophy as a practical tool for life. It outlines how to use philosophy as medicine for the soul, protect one's character from external misfortunes, and perceive the world without illusion. Key concepts include the three disciplines of Stoicism-perception, action, and will-the practice of *Memento Mori* to focus the mind, and the technique of 'contemptuous expressions' to deflate the ego's desire for status. The text emphasizes duty to the community and finding happiness within one's own virtue.

## Summary

It can ruin your life only if it ruins your character. This sentiment, found in Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*, serves as a foundational tenet of Stoicism, a philosophy the Roman Emperor practiced not as an academic exercise, but as a daily code of conduct. Written as a personal diary meant only for himself, *Meditations* offers an unfiltered look into the mind of a leader striving to live virtuously amidst the pressures of ruling an empire. The text distills Stoicism into three core disciplines: perception (seeing things for what they are), action (doing what we can), and will (bearing what we must). Central to Aurelius's teaching is the idea that external events, whether praise or plague, cannot harm our character unless we let them. He encourages us to view philosophy as medicine for the soul, designed to heal suffering and help us endure life's hardships. A recurring theme is the brevity of life and the practice of *Memento Mori*-remembering death-to prioritize what truly matters. Aurelius advises viewing luxury and status with cynical detachment, describing fancy food as 'dead fish' and the Emperor's purple cloak as fabric dyed with shellfish blood. This technique, known as 'contemptuous expressions,' helps strip away the legend encrusting material things. Furthermore, he elevates the collective good, arguing that humans are social beings designed to serve the community like a vine bears grapes. Ultimately, the book teaches that happiness comes from within, dependent on our own virtue and actions rather than external validation.

## Key ideas

### Philosophy Is Medicine for the Soul

Summary: Aurelius argues that philosophy is not an intellectual instructor but a form of medicine-a 'soothing ointment' designed to heal the suffering of human beings and help them navigate the difficulties of life.

Why it matters: Shifting the view of philosophy from abstract theory to practical healing changes how one approaches pain, turning obstacles into opportunities for treatment rather than despair.

Try this: When you face a stressful situation, ask yourself: 'How can I use this as medicine to heal or strengthen my character?' instead of viewing it as mere misfortune.

### Guard Your Character Above All Else

Summary: Character is the only true possession. External calamities, such as plagues or poverty, can take your life or comfort, but they only 'ruin your life' if they corrupt your character.

Why it matters: By separating physical circumstances from moral integrity, you maintain autonomy and self-respect regardless of what happens in the external world.

Try this: In moments of crisis or discomfort, pause and ask: 'Is this threatening my safety or my character?' Focus your energy on protecting your integrity.

### Strip Away the Legend with Contemptuous Expressions

Summary: To maintain mental clarity and avoid being seduced by luxury or status, Aurelius suggests reducing fancy things to their base physical reality-seeing a roast as a dead pig or a purple cloak as shellfish blood.

Why it matters: This practice breaks the spell of marketing and social status, revealing objects as neutral matter rather than sources of happiness, which reduces craving and anxiety.

Try this: The next time you feel envious of a luxury item or status symbol, deconstruct it. Describe a luxury car as 'metal and glass on rubber wheels' or a rich meal as 'processed organic matter.'

### Master the Three Disciplines

Summary: Stoicism can be distilled into three disciplines: Perception (seeing things for what they are), Action (doing what we can), and Will (enduring what we must).

Why it matters: This triage system simplifies complex problems into manageable parts, ensuring you focus energy on what you can change and accept what you cannot.

Try this: When facing a challenge, categorize it: Am I perceiving this correctly? What action can I take? What must I simply accept and endure?

### Let Death Dictate Your Actions

Summary: Aurelius famously reflected that we could leave life right now. This contemplation of death (*Memento Mori*) is intended to determine what we do, say, and think in the present.

Why it matters: Confronting the inevitability of death cuts through trivial worries and clarifies what is truly important, helping to prioritize virtue and meaningful action over fleeting pleasures.

Try this: Remind yourself of your mortality at least twice a day. When making a decision, ask: 'If this were my last day, would I still do this?'

### Serve the Community Without Expectation

Summary: We are social beings meant to serve the community. Aurelius suggests we should do good to others naturally and effortlessly, 'without thinking of the grapes [we] have borne,' just as a vine yields fruit.

Why it matters: Acting for the common good cultivates humility and reinforces the Stoic belief that we are part of a larger whole, reducing the ego's dependence on external validation.

Try this: Perform a kind act or help a colleague today without telling anyone about it and without seeking thanks or recognition.

## Action steps

1. Practice 'Memento Mori' by reflecting on your mortality at least twice a day to guide your decisions.
2. View philosophy not as a subject to study, but as medicine to heal the suffering of life.
3. Apply the 'three disciplines' of Stoicism: See things clearly, do what you can, and endure what you must.
4. Dismiss public praise as 'the clacking of tongues' to maintain indifference to others' opinions.
5. Use 'contemptuous expressions' to deconstruct luxury items (e.g., seeing a roast as a dead animal) to strip away their allure.
6. Remind yourself that difficult people are inevitable, asking: 'Is a world without shameless people possible?' to accept reality.

## This is how to relate to your environment in a stoic way
"If something does not make a person worse in himself, neither does it make his life worse, nor does it harm him without or within."
Stoic philosophers have placed the discussion of ethical issues at the forefront, so they have often been simplified and schematized in the description of both the physical and the natural environment.
Living beings, as interpreted by Marcus Aurelius, fall into three categories:
- plants,
- beings with soul (animals),
- beings with intellect (humans).
The function of plants and animals is to serve intelligent beings, while the purpose of intelligent beings is to
**serve** the **community**.
In short, we are social beings by nature.
Although it is worth keeping in mind the well-being of the community, this does not mean that we should place great emphasis on the actions and opinions of the members of the community.
"It is not events that disturb people, it is their judgements concerning them."
The external environment is most often embodied by people, so the wisdom that was also written by Seneca also applies to our interactions with our peers:
*Happiness comes from within*.
Let the opinions of others not affect our happiness, let us not choose goals based on the likes of others, let recognition not be our main motivation.
Marcus Aurelius says the same thing:
"Anything in any way beautiful derives its beauty from itself and asks nothing beyond itself.
Praise is no part of it, for nothing is made worse or better by praise."
**The source of our happiness is ourselves.
If we can influence our environment, the outcome ultimately depends on us.
Don’t care about factors beyond our control, let’s focus on what we have an impact on.**
