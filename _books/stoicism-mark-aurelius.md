## Summary

It can ruin your life only if it ruins your character. This sentiment, found in Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*, serves as a foundational tenet of Stoicism, a philosophy the Roman Emperor practiced not as an academic exercise, but as a daily code of conduct. Written as a personal diary meant only for himself, *Meditations* offers an unfiltered look into the mind of a leader striving to live virtuously amidst the pressures of ruling an empire. The text distills Stoicism into three core disciplines: perception (seeing things for what they are), action (doing what we can), and will (bearing what we must). Central to Aurelius's teaching is the idea that external events, whether praise or plague, cannot harm our character unless we let them. He encourages us to view philosophy as medicine for the soul, designed to heal suffering and help us endure life's hardships. A recurring theme is the brevity of life and the practice of *Memento Mori*-remembering death-to prioritize what truly matters. Aurelius advises viewing luxury and status with cynical detachment, describing fancy food as 'dead fish' and the Emperor's purple cloak as fabric dyed with shellfish blood. This technique, known as 'contemptuous expressions,' helps strip away the legend encrusting material things. Furthermore, he elevates the collective good, arguing that humans are social beings designed to serve the community like a vine bears grapes. Ultimately, the book teaches that happiness comes from within, dependent on our own virtue and actions rather than external validation.
