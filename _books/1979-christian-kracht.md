## Hook

In a world obsessed with aesthetics and hedonism, one wealthy drifter discovers that the ultimate escape from a meaningless existence is the terrifying surrender of the self to totalitarian ideology.

## Summary

### The Aesthete in the Apocalypse

In 1979, Swiss author Christian Kracht presents a jarring portrait of a man out of time and place. The novel introduces an unnamed, wealthy German interior designer who travels to Tehran with his ex-lover, Christopher. It is the eve of the Islamic Revolution, but the protagonist remains wilfully obtuse, fixated on the cut of a suit or the weave of a fabric while political upheaval rages outside his window. This novel serves as the second part of a triptych that includes *Faserland* and *Ich werde hier sein im Sonnenschein und im Schatten*, all tracing unnamed narrators walking toward their own destruction.

The story begins with a tone of hollow elegance. The narrator and Christopher are remnants of a dying jet-set age, moving through a world that is rapidly collapsing. They attend parties populated by the Western-oriented elite, drinking champagne and discussing Safavid architecture while the Shah's regime teeters on the brink of collapse. Kracht uses this setting to critique the disconnectedness of the Western bourgeoisie, who view the world merely as a backdrop for their aesthetic enjoyment.

#### The Collapse of Pleasure

The initial shield of money and taste proves fragile. Christopher, a figure described as a perverted, Christ-like character, destroys himself through drug abuse. His death in a derelict hospital marks the end of the narrator's hedonistic phase. Stranded and alone, the narrator is forced to confront the reality that his aesthetic existence offers no protection against the chaos of history.

It is at this juncture that the narrator meets a mysterious, almost Mephistophelian figure who encourages him to travel to Tibet. This sets the second half of the novel in motion. The protagonist is no longer seeking pleasure but is instead propelled by a vague desire for deliverance. He travels to the sacred Mount Kailash, intending to circle it, a task traditionally associated with spiritual cleansing. However, Kracht subverts this trope. Instead of finding enlightenment, the narrator is captured by the Chinese army.

#### The Comfort of Captivity

The final section of the book takes place in a forced labor camp in the Lop Nor desert. Here, the novel shifts from the satire of Western decadence to the horror of totalitarianism. The narrator is interned as a supposed Russian spy. He undergoes indoctrination sessions and learns the survival techniques necessary to endure life in the camp.

In a disturbing twist, the narrator adapts to his captivity with alarming ease. He confesses to crimes he did not commit and embraces the ideology of his captors. He admits to having learned Mandarin to spy on and destroy Chinese society. This transformation completes the arc of the novel: the protagonist moves from a worship of aesthetics and Christopher to a submission to religion and finally to ideology.

Critics have noted that the protagonist lacks the capability for resistance. He is a man who can do anything to him, even subdue him to inhuman totalitarianism, because he has no inner core to fight back. He describes himself as a "good prisoner" who always tries to follow the rules. The dystopia of the camp offers him the one thing his life of luxury lacked: structure and a total removal of the burden of self.

#### The Language of Nihilism

Kracht's prose in 1979 is notable for its cold beauty. The author describes scenes of torture, hunger, and degradation with the same elegiac, nonchalant tone he uses to describe dinner parties and designer clothes. For instance, the starvation in the camp is described with horrific clinical detail; prisoners breed maggots on piles of trash for protein, and violence is commonplace. A prisoner is killed during an argument when a stick is drilled through his ear into his brain.

This stylistic choice creates a sense of dissonance. The pop music soundtrack of Devo and Blondie that plays in the early parts of the book is replaced by the brutal reality of the camp, yet the narrator's voice remains eerily consistent. He becomes a shell of a man, a "new man" shaped entirely by the system that imprisons him. The novel ends with two sentences that encapsulate this terrifying transformation: "I have improved. I have never eaten human flesh."

This ending is both a confession of submission and a grim assertion of moral survival. The narrator believes he has "improved" because he has been successfully re-educated, yet his refusal to eat human flesh is the only remnant of his previous identity. It is a slim hope, but a hope nonetheless. As one German critic noted, the final sentence offers an occasion for hope because the protagonist has passed the cruel tests of camp existence, opening the possibility to become a "new person."

#### A Warning Against Escapism

Ultimately, 1979 is a warning about the dangers of regression and the abdication of responsibility. The novel suggests that the protagonist's journey was a search for a father figure or an overarching authority to tell him how to live. Whether it was the aesthetic authority of high society, the religious authority of the revolution, or the political authority of the Maoist camp, the protagonist consistently sought to obliterate his own will.

The book was published shortly after the attacks of September 11, 2001, a date that casts a long shadow over the text. Its exploration of fanaticism, the collision of civilizations, and the emptiness of Western consumerism feels prescient. Kricht shows us that the alternative to a life grounded in reality and personal responsibility is not paradise, but the "hell" of a labor camp where the individual is erased.

The reader is left with the task of reflecting on the events because the narrator is incapable of doing so. He moves through the apocalypse unscathed in his mind, noticing only the superficial while his humanity is stripped away. It is a brutal, brilliant critique of a world that has lost its way, and a terrifying look at how easily the human desire to belong can lead to total self-destruction.

## Key ideas

### The Aesthetic Shield

Summary: The protagonist uses wealth and taste to insulate himself from political reality, focusing on furniture and fashion rather than revolution.

Why it matters: It highlights the disconnect between privileged Western elites and historical catastrophes unfolding around them.

Try this: Notice when you focus on superficial comforts to avoid facing difficult realities or emotional pain.

### Subversion of the Quest

Summary: The narrative inverts the classic journey of self-discovery, leading the protagonist toward the erasure of his identity rather than its fulfillment.

Why it matters: It challenges the reader to question the value of traditional spiritual or physical journeys in a postmodern world.

Try this: Reflect on whether your personal goals are leading to greater autonomy or merely replacing one set of influences

### Submission as Salvation

Summary: The protagonist moves from seeking pleasure in drugs and art to finding relief in the rigid rules of a prison camp.

Why it matters: It illustrates the terrifying allure of totalitarianism for individuals who feel overwhelmed by the burden of freedom and choice.

Try this: Identify areas of your life where you might be abdicating responsibility to authority figures because it feels easier

### The Banality of Horror

Summary: The novel describes torture and indoctrination with the same elegant, detached tone used to describe furniture and dinner parties.

Why it matters: This stylistic choice forces the reader to confront how easily evil can be normalized or ignored.

Summary: The book acts as a funeral for pop literature, using a pop soundtrack to underscore futility rather than celebration.

## Action steps

1. Remain vigilant against the allure of simplistic ideologies that promise to solve complex personal or societal problems.
2. Cultivate critical thinking to resist the temptation to outsource your moral compass to powerful systems or leaders.

4. Use your awareness of history and politics to ground your worldview, rather than hiding behind aesthetic detachment.

1979, the second novel of the Swiss writer Christian Kracht, published in 2001, is an implicit criticism of the culture of Western bourgeoisie.
Both with its title and with its setting in Tehran, especially in the first part, the novel reminds us the 1979 Islamic Revolution in Iran from its start.
Kracht’s novel depicts Tehran as a modern city.
From this perspective, we get to know Tehran as a city where decadent people from all over the world organize jet-set parties, and in the light of this information Tehran as depicted in the novel emerges as a space which is beyond cultures and independent of Eastern identity.
However, the date is 1979 and the city is on the threshold of social and political transformation.
All in all, the novel does not mainly deal with the fact that city, hence the country, is at a political junction.
The novel mainly focuses on an anonymous first person narrator and his partner Christopher who come to the city which is on the verge of the coup.
They came to the city for a degenerate party of some degenerate people.
After this party, which depicts the Western life style in its degenerated from, Christopher dies at a derelict hospital in one of the city’s dark neighbourhoods.
Hence, the anonymous protagonist of 1979 decides to travel East as the other of the West.
As one gets further away from the West, not only the subject with its Western identity, but all other concepts ascribed to the West also die.
Hence, 1979 does not only pinpoint the beginning of a coup in world history, but the novel emerges as a turning point where concepts of space and time are also dissolved as cultural signifiers.

Als der bereits kranke Christopher nach einem Drogenexzess stirbt, wird der Erzähler von einem mysteriösen Rumänen ermutigt, den heiligen Berg Kailash in Tibet zu umrunden.
Dort wird er von der chinesischen Armee aufgegriffen und als vermeintlich russischer Spion in einem Straflager in der Wüste Lop Nor als politischer Häftling interniert.
Die Handlung endet damit, dass der Ich-Erzähler seinen Alltag im Arbeitslager beschreibt.
...
Der Ich-Erzähler schlägt in der Handlung einen zeitlichen Bogen über unterschiedliche politische Revolutionen: Der Roman beginnt am Vorabend der islamischen Revolution des Jahres 1979 im Iran und schließt im Kontext des Nachwirkens der Revolte in China 1949.
Auf dieser Reise wandelt sich der Ich-Erzähler.
Aus einem dekadenten Ästhetik-Schnösel wird ein fanatischer Maoist.
Das literarische Thema der Selbstfindungsreise wird damit ad absurdum geführt.
1979 steht somit ganz im Widerspruch zu Werken wie Hermann Hesses „Siddharta“ oder Paulo Coelhos „Der Alchimist“.
Angesichts der politischen Ereignisse bleibt der Ich-Erzähler für den Leser zu Beginn auffallend unberührt; einige gesellschaftliche und soziale Geschehnisse im Rahmen der islamischen Revolution wie auch im kommunistischen Lager benennt er, wendet sich jedoch sogleich unbedeutenden Details zu, wie z.

Letztlich muss daher der Leser für den Ich-Erzähler die Erlebnisse reflektieren; dieser scheint dazu nicht in der Lage oder lehnt es ab, davon zu erzählen.
Diese Erzählposition ist es auch, mit der „1979“ potentielle Fallen wie eine allzu simple Karikatur des Dandy im Tumult der Geschichte zu vermeiden sucht.
Der Ich-Erzähler schreitet keineswegs souverän durch dieses apokalyptisch übersteigerte Szenario.

Nun hat der Autor das Buch »1979« herausgebracht, eine düstere Endzeit-Erzählung, die ganz anders ist, als jene unpolitische »Generation Golf« erwarten mag.
Ein zerstrittenes schwules Pärchen reist in den letzten Tagen des Schah-Regimes in den Iran und besucht in Teheran eine Party.
Der namenlose Ich-Erzähler, ein Innenarchitekt, beschreibt die Szenerie, teure Stoffe, Kunstschätze, Skulpturen.
Sein Freund Christopher pumpt sich mit Drogen voll, fällt in eine Glastür und stirbt in einem dreckigen Krankenhaus.
»Sein Mund war geöffnet, ich versuchte, ihn zu schließen, aber es gelang mit nicht«, heißt es dazu in dem Buch.
### Von Tibet ins Arbeitslager
Der Erzähler läuft ziellos durch Teheran und stößt auf einen rätselhaften, mephistohaften Dandy, der ihn nach Tibet schickt, wo er mit einer Gruppe von Mönchen den heiligen Berg Kailasch umrundet.
Auf dem chinesischen Gebiet wird er von Soldaten verhaftet, die ihn für einen russischen Spion halten und ihn erst in ein Umerziehungs- und dann in ein Arbeitslager bringen.
Albtraumhafte Szenen aus dem Lager, die an Cynthia Ozicks Auschwitz-Beschreibungen erinnern, folgen - Qual, Folter, Hunger.
Ein Gefangener wird bei einem Streit von anderen Häftlingen mit einem Stäbchen, das sie ihm durch das Ohr in den Kopf bohren, getötet.
Gegen den Hunger züchten die Männer auf den Kotbergen des Mülls Maden - wegen des Proteins.
»1979« endet offen mit den lapidaren Sätzen: »Ich habe mich gebessert.
Ich habe nie Menschenfleisch gegessen.«
### »Unbegreiflich, drohend«
Seltsam enthoben stehen die drei Teile der Erzählung nebeneinander und hinterlassen ein Gefühl der Leere.
Alles wirkt »seriously« verstörend, wie es der Ich-Erzähler selbst nennen würde, und es passt auf fast unheimliche Weise zu der düsteren Stimmung nach den Terroranschlägen vom 11.
September.
»Autoren: Nichts wird je wieder gut«, titelte der »Spiegel«.
Elke Heidenreich lobt darin Krachts Text, der »wie ein dunkler Granitblock in der Landschaft unserer Herbstliteratur liegt.
Hart, kalt schön, unbegreiflich, drohend.« Die hohe Zeit der pop-literarischen Schnellschüsse könnte mit »1979« vorbei sein.
Christian Kracht: 1979
