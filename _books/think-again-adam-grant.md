## Hook

In a world that rewards certainty, discover why the ability to rethink and unlearn is the crucial skill for adaptation, innovation, and success.

## Overview

*Think Again* by Adam Grant is a guide to mastering the skill of rethinking in a world that often prioritizes conviction over doubt. Grant illustrates how we can let go of outdated views, embrace the joy of being wrong, and use conflict constructively to innovate. By thinking like scientists, practicing confident humility, and building cultures of learning, we can improve our decision-making, relationships, and organizations.

## Summary

In a rapidly changing world, the ability to rethink and unlearn is just as important as intelligence. In *Think Again*, Adam Grant explores the art of rethinking-how to question our own opinions, open the minds of others, and foster environments that encourage learning over performance. Grant argues that we often fall into the mental traps of acting like preachers, prosecutors, or politicians, defending our beliefs, proving others wrong, or seeking approval. Instead, we should think like scientists: testing our views, updating our understanding based on new evidence, and remaining curious. The book highlights the dangers of overconfidence and the benefits of confident humility, suggesting that finding joy in being wrong accelerates learning. Grant also addresses the specific dynamics of conflict, distinguishing between relationship conflict (toxic and personal) and task conflict (constructive and idea-focused). When trying to change minds, he advocates for motivational interviewing and communicating respect rather than condemnation. Finally, Grant examines organizational cultures, contrasting performance cultures-which hide mistakes-with learning cultures, which embrace them as opportunities for growth.

## Key ideas

### Think Like a Scientist

Summary: Grant identifies three common modes of thinking that prevent us from rethinking: the preacher (defending sacred beliefs), the prosecutor (proving others wrong), and the politician (winning approval). To truly rethink, we must adopt the mindset of a scientist. Scientists form hypotheses, seek evidence to disprove them, and update their views when the data changes, rather than clinging to convictions regardless of the facts.

Why it matters: Thinking like a scientist allows for flexibility and personal growth, whereas the other three modes entrench us in outdated beliefs and hinder decision-making.

Try this: The next time you feel strongly about an issue, try to prove yourself wrong rather than right. Ask, 'What evidence would change my mind?'

### The Joy of Being Wrong

Summary: Balancing overconfidence and impostor syndrome is 'confident humility'-trusting in your own capacity to learn while recognizing your current limitations. Grant advises anchoring your identity in your values (like honesty or curiosity) rather than your opinions. This way, changing your mind doesn't feel like an attack on your character, but an evolution of your understanding.

Why it matters: When we find joy in being wrong, we view mistakes as opportunities to learn. This allows us to correct course faster and avoid the pitfalls of rigid certainty.

Try this: Separate your sense of self from your specific views. Base your identity on your values, so you can change an opinion without feeling like you've lost yourself.

### The Good Fight

Summary: Not all conflict is bad. Grant distinguishes between relationship conflict (personal attacks and personality clashes) and task conflict (clashes of ideas and perspectives). Relationship conflict is toxic and hinders progress, while task conflict drives creativity and better decision-making. A 'challenge network' of people who disagree with you is often more valuable than a support network of 'yes men'.

Why it matters: Focusing on task conflict allows teams to innovate and solve problems without damaging personal relationships or creating a toxic environment.

Try this: When discussing a problem, frame the conflict as a debate about the task or idea, not a disagreement about the people. Ask, 'How can we make this idea better?'

### Motivational Interviewing

Summary: Changing minds requires humility and curiosity, not adversarial arguments. Grant suggests using 'motivational interviewing,' a technique where you ask open-ended questions to help people discover their own reasons for change. Finding common ground and listening with respect is far more effective than simply telling someone they are wrong.

Why it matters: Adversarial approaches usually make people defensive. Collaborative techniques allow individuals to maintain their dignity while arriving at new conclusions on their own terms.

Try this: Instead of lecturing a stubborn person, ask open-ended questions like, 'Why do you feel that way?' or 'What would it take for you to reconsider?'

### Creating a Culture of Learning

Summary: Organizations often fall into 'performance cultures' where results are prioritized, mistakes are hidden, and 'I don't know' is a sign of weakness. Grant advocates for 'cultures of learning,' where the goal is improvement. In these environments, leaders admit their own mistakes, embrace 'best practices' as temporary, and reward rethinking over rigid consistency.

Why it matters: Cultures of learning foster psychological safety, allowing members to take risks, innovate, and address systemic issues without fear of blame.

Try this: If you are a leader, publicly admit a recent mistake or a gap in your knowledge to show that rethinking is safe and encouraged.

## Action steps

1. Adopt the mindset of a scientist by forming hypotheses, seeking disconfirming evidence, and holding assumptions loosely.
2. Base your identity on your values rather than your opinions to allow for flexibility without compromising your character.
3. Differentiate between task conflict and relationship conflict, focusing debates on the problem rather than the people involved.
4. Use motivational interviewing techniques, such as asking open-ended questions, to help others find their own motivation to change.
5. Build a culture of learning where admitting 'I don't know' or 'I was wrong' is viewed as a sign of strength rather than weakness.

Creating Cultures of Learning
Grant distinguishes between cultures of performance and cultures of learning.
In performance cultures, the focus is on results, achievement, attribution, and credit.
This can undermine learning and improvement, hiding mistakes, blame, and unethical choices.
Grant outlines NASA’s performance culture and the consequences and the slow, difficult, and inconsistent shifts toward more of a culture of learning.
In cultures of learning, people can disagree without fear of consequence, which helps them disagree without being disagreeable.
We can better focus on the task conflict and not be tempted to fall into relationship conflict.
We can give up “best practices” because they are stagnant and limited and embrace improvement, innovation, and new knowledge and understanding.
Leaders acknowledge and role model openness to feedback, mistakes, and their need for ongoing learning and growth.
In learning cultures, “I don’t know” and “I was wrong” are seen as admirable and courageous leadership qualities.
In performance cultures, they are seen as signs of incompetence and weakness.

   In Chapter 1 of *Think Again*, Grant invites readers to adopt a scientist’s mindset in their everyday lives.
Rethinkers by profession, scientists routinely update their views based on new data.
Most of us, though, operate as preachers, prosecutors, and politicians.
In preacher mode, people defend their beliefs even without evidence.
Those in prosecutor mode try to prove others wrong.
And politicians work on persuading others to support their views.
Only in scientist mode are people willing to rethink by remaining curious and testing hypotheses.
To demonstrate that even visionary thinkers are likely to fail if they can’t rethink, Grant relates the story of Mike Lazaridis, inventor of the BlackBerry.
This smartphone was wildly successful for a few years in the late 2000s and early 2010s before suddenly fading into obscurity.
Lazaridis was so resistant to the idea of a touchscreen that he would not rethink his product.
Steve Jobs, co-founder of Apple, by contrast, was saved from a similar fate by his team’s ability to rethink.
Jobs initially resisted putting a phone into Apple’s top product, the iPod, but his team urged him to reconsider his own convictions.
The iPhone was the result of Jobs’s ability to rethink his pet product, something Lazaridis was unable to do.
Read more about Main Idea #1: How individuals benefit from rethinking.
In Chapters 2 and 3, Grant explores the surprisingly detrimental role played by confidence and invites readers to find joy in being wrong.
Grant argues that many people struggle to understand what they cannot see about themselves.
Indeed, research shows that overconfidence is most common in those who lack competence.
This contradiction, known as the Dunning-Kruger effect, is why armchair quarterbacks usually know little about football.
Somewhere between impostor syndrome (feeling like a fraud despite one’s positive achievements) and armchair quarterback syndrome lies a sweet spot that Grant calls confident humility.
Those with confident humility trust their own strengths while being aware of their weaknesses.
Grant suggests that if people base their identity on their values rather than their opinions, they are more likely to remain open-minded enough to embrace being wrong.
This is important because it offers an opportunity to learn.
Grant advises readers to treat challenges to their opinions as opportunities to evolve.
The faster someone recognizes when they are wrong, Grant argues, the faster they can move toward getting it right.
In Chapter 4, Grant asserts that while conflict in relationships is generally unproductive, so-called task conflict can be beneficial to performance.
Task conflict involves clashes about ideas and opinions rather than personal animosity.
According to Grant, multiple studies have linked task conflict to higher creativity and smarter choices.
He uses the example of the squabbling Wright brothers to show task conflict at its most productive.
Disagreeable people force others to question and rethink, and are therefore ideal members of a challenge network, which is often more useful than a support network.
Grant notes that too many leaders shield themselves from task conflict by surrounding themselves with yes men.
Those who engage with their critics become stronger leaders, he believes.
Grant contends that task conflicts are “good fights” because the tension is intellectual rather than emotional.
However, when task conflict spills over into relationship conflict it stops being productive.
To avoid this, Grant suggests framing a dispute as a debate rather than a disagreement.
In a good fight, he concludes, one’s adversary is not a foil, but a propeller.
With twin propellers spinning in different directions, ideas can take flight.
