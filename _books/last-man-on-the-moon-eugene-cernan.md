## Summary

### The Last Man on the Moon

Eugene Cernan’s memoir, co-authored with Don Davis, serves as a definitive account of the final era of America’s race to the Moon. As the commander of Apollo 17, Cernan holds the unique distinction of being the last human being to leave a footprint on the lunar surface. However, this book is far more than a technical log of spaceflight; it is a deeply human story of obsession, sacrifice, and the random currents of fate that determine who makes history and who is left behind. Cernan writes with a minimum of technical jargon, offering a close-up view of the personal price paid by the astronauts and their families during the height of the Cold War space race.

#### The Crucible of Tragedy

Cernan chose to open his narrative with the devastating Apollo 1 fire, a decision rooted in the profound impact the event had on his life and the program. The loss of Gus Grissom, Ed White, and his close friend Roger Chaffee was a watershed moment. At the time, NASA was under immense pressure to meet President Kennedy’s deadline of reaching the Moon before the end of the decade. Cernan admits that the culture of cost and schedule compromises led to shortcuts, such as testing the spacecraft with 100 percent oxygen at high pressure on the launchpad-a recipe for disaster.

The aftermath of the fire forced a complete regrouping of the space agency. It compelled engineers and astronauts to take a step back, review protocols, and prioritize safety over speed. Cernan argues that this tragedy was perhaps the single most important factor in eventually reaching the Moon successfully. It renewed the team’s spirit and conviction, transforming a bureaucratic race into a moral crusade to honor their fallen colleagues by doing the job right. Without the painful lessons of Apollo 1, Cernan believes further losses would have been inevitable.

#### The Spacewalk from Hell

Before commanding a lunar mission, Cernan faced his own harrowing brush with death during the Gemini 9 mission. What was supposed to be a routine extravehicular activity (EVA) turned into a terrifying struggle. Lacking proper handholds and stabilization training, Cernan found himself wrestling with his own umbilical cord in the vacuum of space.

He describes this experience, famously dubbed "The Spacewalk from Hell," with visceral clarity. The tether tangled around him, cavorting with a life of its own like a snake, while he spun uncontrollably. Physically exhausted and blinded by sweat that would not wipe away, he was absolutely helpless. He ultimately survived this ordeal, but it served as a critical lesson for the program. It highlighted that EVA training was insufficient, paving the way for Buzz Aldrin to develop new rigorous training techniques that became the standard for future missions.

#### Ambition and the Apollo 17 Mission

Cernan’s narrative reveals a personality defined by an insatiable ambition to excel. He did not want to merely be an astronaut; he wanted to be the best. When assigned to Apollo 17, his goal was multifaceted: to pilot the mission better than any other, to lead his crew effectively, and to walk on the Moon himself. This drive often painted him as arrogant, abrasive, and self-absorbed to his peers, but it was also the engine that propelled him to the pinnacle of his profession.

The book details the long and arduous career path required to achieve those few steps on the lunar surface. From his early days as a naval aviator struggling through the academic rigors of Purdue University to the intense training alongside contemporaries like Tom Stafford, Cernan’s journey was marked by relentless pressure. He candidly discusses the unconventional work-life balance of an astronaut, embracing the mundane routines of fatherhood while simultaneously relishing the celebrity and fanfare that came with the job.

#### Fate and the Final Footprint

A recurring theme in Cernan’s story is the role of fate in the lives of astronauts. He recounts the tragic crash of a jet that killed Charlie Bassett and Elliott See, the prime crew for Gemini 9. This random accident of fortune cleared the path for Cernan and Stafford to take their place on the mission. Cernan is philosophical about these deaths, seeing neither plan nor order in them, only the brutal physics of space travel and the randomness of life. He often visits Arlington National Cemetery, asking why his friends were taken instead of him. It was fate, he concludes, that picked Neil Armstrong to be first on the Moon, just as it was fate that made Cernan the last.

When Cernan finally stepped off the ladder of the lunar module, he was overcome with a sense of poetry and wonder that contrasted with his usual military pragmatism. His famous words upon leaving-"As I take man's last step from the surface, back home for some time to come-but we believe not too long into the future-'d like to just say what I believe history will record: that America's challenge of today has forged man's destiny of tomorrow"-reflected a profound spiritual shift. He notes that going to the Moon changed him, shaping his view on life and humanity in ways he could not have anticipated.

#### A Legacy of Inspiration

In the years since his return, no human has traveled further into space than low-Earth orbit. Cernan views this not as an end, but as a pause. He has taken on the role of a storyteller, aiming to inspire the next generation to dream big. He believes that while not every life mission involves going to the Moon, each requires the same ferocious focus and commitment that defined the Apollo era. He speaks to children, encouraging them to become doctors, teachers, engineers, and scientists, urging them to believe they can do things others say they cannot. For Cernan, the legacy of the last man on the Moon is not just a footprint in the lunar dust, but the enduring power of human resilience and the courage to reach beyond the known world.
