## Summary

### A Utopia on the Brink

In the year 2454, the world has achieved a state of technological abundance that effectively eliminates want. Flying cars can transport anyone anywhere on the globe in under two hours, rendering the concepts of borders and nation-states obsolete. In this post-scarcity future, humanity has organized itself into seven massive Hives-global, borderless nations based on shared philosophies, professions, and interests rather than geography. Citizens choose their Hive upon reaching adulthood, effectively selecting the legal code and cultural values that will govern their lives.

This is the stage for Ada Palmer’s *Too Like the Lightning*, a novel that is less a straightforward thriller and more a sophisticated philosophical inquiry. The story is narrated by Mycroft Canner, a convicted criminal serving a sentence of lifelong utility. Unlike traditional prisons, the 25th century condemns its worst offenders to Servicer status, forcing them to perform whatever labor society demands. Mycroft, a brilliant polymath, is uniquely positioned within this world. He serves the elite Saneer-Weeksbooth bash’-a familial unit within the Humanist Hive responsible for managing the world’s transportation network.

But beneath the polished surface of this rational, peaceful society lies profound tension. The Saneer-Weeksbooth bash’ harbors a secret that could shatter the world: Bridger, a young boy with the power to make his imagination reality. He can bring inanimate objects to life effortlessly, a supernatural ability in a world that has strictly outlawed religious belief to prevent past wars from recurring. Bridger’s existence is known only to a select few, including Mycroft and Carlyle Foster, a sensayer-a state-appointed spiritual counselor who tends to the private needs of a populace forbidden from public worship.

#### The Architecture of a New World

Palmer’s world-building is intricate, drawing heavily from the 18th-century Enlightenment to project a future that feels both alien and historically resonant. The novel does not simply list technologies; it explores the societal shifts those technologies necessitate. With instant global travel, geographic nationalism is impossible. Instead, people form collective families called bashes’, which function as communes of shared interest rather than blood relations. These groups provide the social fabric previously supplied by nations and traditional nuclear families.

The Hives themselves represent distinct answers to human organization. There is the Masonic Empire, which mimics a Roman dictatorship; the Cousins, who focus on care and education; the Humanists, who value excellence and sportsmanship; Mitsubishi, representing commerce; the European Union, preserving heritage; Gordian, focused on transhumanism; and Utopia, dedicated to innovation and space.

In this society, labor requires only 20 hours a week, leaving the populace to pursue vocation and avocation with equal intensity. However, this freedom has led to a complex culture of decadence and intellectualism. The novel portrays a society that is morally complex, where the pursuit of pleasure and high philosophy coexist. Privacy is minimal in a world of ubiquitous information, yet the greatest taboos remain: one cannot discuss gender in public, nor can one discuss religion outside the confidential confines of a sensayer’s booth.

#### Philosophy as the Engine of Plot

While there is a plot-involving the theft of a Seven-Ten list, a ranking of the world’s most powerful people that implicates the Saneer-Weeksbooth bash’ in a conspiracy-the narrative momentum is generated primarily by dialogue and debate. Mycroft Canner addresses the reader directly, often adopting the tone of an 18th-century historian. He is an unreliable narrator, a self-confessed sinner hiding his own crimes and motives, which colors every description of the utopia around him.

The book is often described as a "Conversation" rather than a story. It explicitly references the works of Voltaire, Diderot, and Rousseau, using their questions about cultural relativism, hierarchy, and justice to interrogate this future society. The characters debate whether the utilitarian calculus used to maintain peace is truly just, or if it merely suppresses the chaos of the human spirit. The character of Carlyle Foster serves as a vehicle for these theological explorations wrestling with how to counsel a soul in a world that denies the soul’s public existence.

#### The Wild Card of the Supernatural

The mystery of Bridger strikes at the heart of this rationalist experiment. If a child can manifest miracles, then the foundational assumptions of the Enlightenment-that the world is governed by knowable, natural laws-are thrown into question. Bridger is a liability and a holy terror. The bash’ protects him not out of mere kindness, but because he represents a power that no Hive can control. His ability to conjure life-saving drugs or animate toy soldiers introduces a variable of pure chaos into a system designed for perfect equilibrium.

This supernatural element juxtaposes the hard science fiction of the setting with fantasy, forcing the characters to confront the limitations of their philosophy. The narrative asks whether a society built on reason can accommodate the unreasonable. Can the Hives, which rely on the predictability of human behavior and economics, survive an entity that operates on wish fulfillment?

#### The Fragility of Peace

As the investigation into the Seven-Ten list theft widens, the political stability of the Hives begins to fracture. The novel suggests that this utopia is perpetually on the brink of war, held together only by the delicate balance of reputation and the fear of mutual destruction. The elites, including Mycroft, engage in high-stakes maneuvering where information is the ultimate weapon. The theft is not just a crime but a triggering event that threatens to undo the global order.

Mycroft’s role is central and paradoxical. He is both a Servicer, at the bottom of the social hierarchy, and an intimate of the most powerful people on Earth. He is a monster who protects a child-saint. Through his eyes, we see that this future is not a solved problem but a constant negotiation between competing evils. The novel does not offer easy answers. Instead, it presents a "baroque" tableau of a world that is heaven and hell simultaneously, depending on one’s perspective.

#### Key Themes

**Elimination of Gender:** Society has moved past gender distinctions, which are now taboo in public. However, Mycroft frequently genders the people he describes in his narration, a quirk that highlights the persistence of these concepts in the human mind despite their social eradication.

**The Repression of Religion:** To end wars caused by dogma, religious practice was privatized and monopolized by sensayers. This creates a society where spiritual hunger is acknowledged but strictly controlled, creating a pressure cooker of belief.

**Justice and Utility:** The legal system prioritizes the utility of the criminal over retribution. Mycroft Canner is the ultimate example-a man who committed unspeakable crimes yet is indispensable to the functioning of the world.

*Too Like the Lightning* is a challenging but rewarding read. It requires patience, as the plot advances slowly beneath the weight of its ideas. Yet for the reader willing to engage with its complex prose and historical allusions, it offers a profound meditation on what humanity might become when we are freed from the struggles of scarcity but left to battle the demons of our own nature.

Summary: Lawbreakers are not imprisoned but sentenced to serve as utility workers, forced to perform any task required of society.

Summary: A young boy named Bridger possesses the supernatural ability to bring inanimate objects to life through sheer will.

Why it matters: Bridger represents a destabilizing wild card in a planned, rational world, introducing metaphysical questions about the nature of reality.

### The Value of Reputation

Summary: In a world without material scarcity, reputation acts as the primary currency, exemplified by the coveted Seven-Ten list of influencers.

Why it matters: This shifts the basis of power from wealth to social standing, creating new forms of competition and vulnerability.

There is no scarcity, information (and surveillance) is ubiquitous, people spend their lives on what they want to, freely travel wherever they desire, and the resulting moral decay of the society is off the charts.
That is not to say that there is no need for labor.
People still have employment, but it’s no longer defining them, and instead they spend more time and energy on their hobbies.
There are exceptions, though.
For instance, there are the Servicers, criminals who have been convicted of very serious crimes but given a second chance by being forced to work in any job that is assigned to them, in exchange for food.
One such servicer is Mycroft Canner, the narrator of this book.
He is a brilliant polymath, versed in multiple languages, and with excellent mathematical skills.
Thanks to this, and his association to the Emperor, he is sought after by the elites who require more sensitive work than just shoveling muck.
This puts him into the central position for this story, which is why he had been tasked to compile a history of the entire affair and present it to the reader, whom he often directly addresses.
Even with his access to information, he is not the most reliable narrator, and sometimes even relies on others to write certain chapters.
To understand the significance of the criminal case that is presented in this book, one must first know the political structure of the future.
Nation-states were replaced by seven Hives.
People are free to apply to a hive or stay hiveless.
Within the hives, people form familial units, bashes, which may consist of a larger number of adults and children, with or without any blood relations between them.
The hive leaders are very familiar with each other and prefer to cooperate rather than plot against one another, but over time the balance of power has been slipping towards some of the hives, so a corrective action may be in order.
Mycroft is often employed by the Saneer-Weeksbooth bash of the Humanist Hive.
This bash is responsible for routing most of the traffic in the world and ensuring the safety of all cars.
However, the bash, unbeknownst to even its own members, also harbors a child that has the power to give life to inanimate objects and to turn anything tangible.
Bridger, the child, has already turned a squad of toy soldiers into his bodyguards, and he’s been supplying pharmaceutical labs with life-saving drugs that he’s conjured.
Mycroft, along with the bash member Thisbe, resolve to keep the child’s identity a secret and protect him until he comes of age.
In this setting, someone has stolen the Mitsubishi Hive Seven-Ten list and implicated the S-W bash in the crime.
These lists, published every year by the most reputable newspapers, contain the names of the seven most influential people in the world and three up-and-commers.
In a post-scarcity world, reputation becomes a commodity, and so these lists are carefully guarded until publication.
The theft triggers an investigation of the bash, but also sets in motion a set of events that threatens the stability of the entire Hive system.
Hive leaders begin suspecting each other, key investigators turn rogue, Mycroft’s identity as the world’s most despicable mass murderer gets exposed, Bridger gets hunted, and Mycroft himself becomes a pawn in the power plays and gets confined away from the action.
For a book of such an enormous scope, the storyline may sound rather thin and short, and it indeed could be summarized in a few paragraphs.
It is, in essence, the story of Bridger, and the story of the theft.
The two threads are never really interconnected, and the Bridger story, which ostensibly is what Mycroft’s accounting is about, is actually pushed into the background by the theft.
The story of the theft takes several sharp turns, and a reader may not even realize that he’s been heading into an entirely different direction until the book comes to a close.
What appeared to be a simple theft or even a prank turns into a global conspiracy, which may threaten the future of mankind.
Even this is drowned out, though, by the bulk of the novel: worldbuilding and classic literature and philosophy.
What happens to a society that seemingly has everything?
They lapse into decadence.
Free to do as they please (unless laws are broken), people can pursue their base instincts and loftiest intellectual pursuits at once.
As a result, you have a sex-crazed society obsessed by image, which is deeply involved with philosophical thought and classic literature.
This is further enhanced by an interesting quirk of the worldbuilding: religion, being declared the root of all wars and human suffering, has been outlawed.
It is replaced by the Cousins Hive, whose members are the Sensayers, a cross between a psychiatrists and philosophers, who take confessions and offer advice.
One of the sensayers, Carlyle Foster, is a central figure in the book, and through him we get treated to philosophical discussions, primarily featuring French Enlightenment philosophers like Voltaire and Rousseau.
And, since this is a sexually depraved (or free) world, Marquis de Sade also plays a major role, from philosophical perspective.
In addition to philosophy, the book is full of mythology, as well as past and future history.
Palmer succeeds to create a plausible future history without getting into too many technical details, and instead she focuses on the societal effects.
Sometimes she may get overboard, for instance with callous destruction of property for the sake of art, and occasionally she becomes too tied up in reconstructing the ancient world in the future, but for the most part the three central elements, philosophy, mythology and history, are very well balanced and never overwhelming.
...
Palmer’s obsession with genders and gendered language adds another, very intriguing, layer to the story, which among other things gives the reader a much greater freedom to imagine characters as he likes.
...
The only downside of the book is that it’s incomplete.
It has been envisioned as the first part of a duology, so the action stops in the middle, and the conclusion is missing.
...
What I can do, however, is to fully appreciate the cultural, societal, and philosophical layers of the story.
*Too Like the Lightning* is a must-read book for everyone with even passing interest in European history and culture, as well as appreciation of a more literary science fiction, full of philosophy and compelling, well-developed characters.
The book will not make it easy for the reader: it is dense, with multiple and often conflicting writing styles, and a language that varies from the 18 th century to a truly futuristic one.
...
But ultimately, this novel delivers one of the best fleshed-out future worlds, beautifully weird characters and a storyline that twists and turns and surprises in nearly every chapter.
For me personally, this book is one of the greatest literary achievements in science fiction of the 21 st century.
