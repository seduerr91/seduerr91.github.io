## Summary

### Finding Your Place in a World Not Built for You

Anna Zoe Quirke’s debut novel, *Something to Be Proud Of*, is a vibrant and necessary addition to the young adult landscape. Set in the north of England, the story follows Imogen Quinn, a chaotic bisexual 16-year-old who uses she/they pronouns and is autistic. After a disastrous experience at a nearby city’s Pride festival-where sensory overload leads to a panic attack and abandonment by so-called friends-Imogen decides to take action. She sets out to create a Pride event that is genuinely accessible to everyone, sparking a journey of activism, friendship, and self-discovery.

#### The spark of Activism

The narrative catalyst is Imogen’s rejection of the status quo. At a standard Pride parade, the loud music, flashing lights, and dense crowds prove to be an inescapable sensory nightmare. Rather than accepting that she simply does not belong in those spaces, Imogen channels her frustration into organization. She marches into her school administrator’s office to demand the formation of an activist society.

Her primary goal is clear: she wants to build something that works for neurodivergent and disabled queer people. This motivation is deeply personal. As Quirke notes in interviews, the story is written for anyone who has ever felt like the world was not designed for them. Imogen’s passion is palpable; she is a character who cares deeply and refuses to 'give a shit' about polite silence when it comes to injustice.

#### An Unlikely Alliance

To bring her vision to life, Imogen needs members. She strategically targets Ollie Armstrong, the school’s openly gay soccer captain. Ollie is white and Japanese, popular, and seemingly has it all together. However, his reality is fraught with tension. He is dealing with the painful fallout of his parents’ recent announcement of their divorce and is shouldering heavy responsibilities at home, including caring for his younger sister.

Initially, Ollie agrees to join the Ardenpool Youth Activist Society only begrudgingly. He is sceptical of Imogen’s intensity and consumed by his own domestic struggles. But as the duo begins to collaborate, a beautiful friendship forms. Refreshingly, while a romance subplot exists, the novel centers their platonic bond. Their relationship evolves from a transactional alliance into a genuine support system where they can be their authentic selves.

#### A Diverse Cast of Allies

Imogen and Ollie are soon joined by a 'dream team' of classmates who bring their own grievances and skills to the table. The group includes:

*   **The Head Girl:** A seemingly perfect student who reveals her own frustrations with the establishment.
*   **Louisa:** A Black student with Ehlers-Danlos syndrome who often uses a wheelchair, bringing a crucial perspective on physical accessibility.
*   **Creatives and Bakers:** Musicians, artists, and a star baker who help with the logistics and flair of their planned events.

Together, this motley crew forms a family unit that feels unbreakable. They tackle issues ranging from the school’s refusal to fund their initiatives to the town council’s failure to keep environmental promises. They also push for practical changes, such as the implementation of gender-neutral bathrooms to support non-binary students.

#### Navigating Systemic Barriers

The club encounters realistic and frustrating setbacks. Readers see the demoralizing effects of administrative incompetence and prejudice. An unsupportive headteacher reallocates funds the students raised themselves, and the group must navigate the bureaucracy of a town council that does not follow through on its word. These conflicts drive the plot and provide a stark look at how power dynamics function in institutions meant to serve young people.

Quirke does not shy away from the heavy emotional toll these battles take. The writing occasionally veers into the didactic as it explains specific accessibility needs, but this serves an educational purpose. It highlights the exhaustion of constantly having to advocate for one's basic right to exist in a space. For Imogen, this is compounded by complicated relationships with her father, who initially dismisses her needs, telling her she must accept there are things 'normal' people do that she cannot.

#### Growth and Self-Acceptance

Beyond the activism, the novel is a poignant coming-of-age story for both protagonists. Ollie’s arc is defined by his search for answers regarding his parents' divorce and his exploration of his own gender identity. He learns to deal with his anger in healthy ways and becomes more open to expressing himself. Imogen, meanwhile, grows in confidence. She finds people who celebrate her for exactly who she is, not in spite of her autism, but because of it.

A particularly moving theme is the idea that one deserves to be loved *because* of who they are. Through their friendship, Ollie learns that love comes in all forms, and Imogen learns to stop taking anyone's 'shit.' The book emphasizes the importance of finding one's 'people'-a community that uplifts and accommodates you.

#### A Rallying Cry for Joy

Ultimately, *Something to Be Proud Of* is a celebration of queer joy and resilience. It balances the heavy themes of discrimination and family strife with humor, wit, and high-energy scenes like a charity drag football game. Quirke’s background as a librarian and autistic author shines through in the authentic representation of neurodivergence and the clear love for the characters.

The story serves as a hopeful rallying cry. It reminds readers that they have the power to create change and that building a more inclusive world is a worthy fight. For young readers, especially those at the intersections of queer and disabled identities, this book offers a comforting hug and a powerful reminder: they deserve a world that is designed for them, not one they have to constantly fight to survive in.

## Key ideas

### The Necessity of Accessible Queer Spaces

Summary: Imogen starts the activist club to create a Pride event that accommodates sensory needs, highlighting the frequent exclusion of disabled people in queer communities.

Why it matters: It underscores that true inclusion requires intentional design and that neurodivergent individuals deserve to celebrate without sensory overload.

Try this: Consider how physical spaces can be adjusted to be more welcoming for those with sensory processing differences.

### Friendship Over Romance

Summary: While romantic subplots exist, the core emotional anchor of the story is the deep, platonic bond that develops between Imogen and Ollie.

Why it matters: It centers a narrative where friendship is the driving force of personal growth and activism, offering a refreshing dynamic in

Try this: Value and nurture your platonic friendships with the same intentionality you might give a romantic partnership.

### Parentification and Hidden Struggles

Summary: Ollie wrestles with the burden of his parents' impending divorce and his responsibilities caring for his younger sister, masking his misery behind social popularity.

Why it matters: It reveals the complex reality of 'parentified' teens who support their families while dealing with their own emotional turmoil.

Try this: Offer specific, practical help to friends dealing with family crises rather than vague offers of support.

### Challenging Autistic Stereotypes

Summary: Imogen is portrayed as a chaotic, passionate, and empathetic individual who defies the 'quiet genius' or unemotional tropes often associated with autism.

Why it matters: It provides vital representation for autistic readers, showing that autism manifests in loud, funny, and deeply caring ways.

Try this: Challenge your own assumptions about how neurodivergent people 'should' behave or present themselves.

### The Power of Youth Activism

Summary: The Ardenpool Youth Activist Society demonstrates that teenagers have the agency to demand changes from school leadership and local councils.

Why it matters: It empowers young readers to believe they can effect change in their environments even when facing bureaucratic hurdles.

## Action steps

1. Evaluate the accessibility of events you attend or organize, considering factors like noise levels and quiet spaces.
2. Practice patience with friends who may be masking their struggles, as Ollie did with his family issues.
3. Speak up about unjust policies or lack of inclusion in your school or workplace, even when it is uncomfortable.
4. Seek out stories by queer and neurodivergent authors to better understand experiences different from your own.

7. [Something to be Proud Of by Anna Zoe Quirke](https://worldssmallestlibrary.wordpress.com/2024/03/10/something-to-be-proud-of-by-anna-zoe-quirke/)
   worldssmallestlibrary.wordpress.com · 2024-03-10
   A humorous and inclusive title, this is a book that I’m glad I bent my rules for.
Addressing some very important issues that affect not just the book’s target audience but everyone in our society, this is a thought-provoking story that deserves to be widely read and will no doubt inspire many young people to take a stand against things that they know to be wrong.
Imogen finds out the hard way that Jen and Hannah are not the friends she thought they were when, overwhelmed by the noise at her first Pride event she needs to escape and is soon abandoned by them.
Texting her father to come and collect her, she is hurt when he makes it clear that he is missing the cricket to do so and rather than being supportive, tells her that she shouldn’t have gone.
Patiently explaining to him that she is not the only neurodivergent queer person in the world, she cannot believe it when he replies that she needs to accept that there are some things that she cannot do like
*normal* people, leaving her upset and furious.
Heading to the sanctuary of her bedroom, Imogen starts to formulate a plan and the following morning, bursts into headteacher Ms Greenacre’s office to try to put it into action.
Explaining that she would like to start an activist society, she refuses to be deterred by the head’s attempts to get her to back down and sets about meeting the conditions of setting up the group.
Marching up to Ollie, the popular and openly gay captain of the football team, to try to enlist his support, she refuses to be put off by his initial reluctance to join in, and before long has not only recruited him but several others, enabling the creation of the society.
Soon, the group have a plan of action but when their attempts to push the school into being more inclusive anger Ms Greenacre, it looks as though the society has been stopped before it really got started.
Can Imogen, Ollie and the others find a way to bring about the changes that they have asked for?
And can they go further and make Imogen’s dreams of an inclusive Pride event reality?
Although the world is a more inclusive place than it was when I was Imogen’s age, it still has a long way to go and for young adults like Imogen, standing up for what they believe in will hopefully bring about further change.
Although she initially chooses Ollie to be a part of the group because he is popular and holds quite a lot of influence over their peers – especially the boys – very quickly they become the best of friends bonding not only as a result of their both being a part of the LGBTQ+ community, but because they genuinely enjoy one another’s company.
In Ollie, Imogen finds someone who not only accepts that she is autistic but actively tries to understand her and how best to support her needs – something that other friends, like Jen and Hannah, and sometimes even her own family have not done.
In return, Ollie finds a sympathetic ear in Imogen – someone in whom he can confide following the separation and impending divorce of his parents and someone with whom he can experiment with both his masculinity and femininity.
Their deepening friendship is an absolute joy to watch throughout the book and for
*all* of those reading this, it is not just a cracking read that will hugely entertain but one that will lead to a deeper understanding of difference and will encourage young adults to express themselves in whichever way they feel best for them.
In common with many other recent titles, this has been written by an author who is themself autistic and it is so lovely to see that authentic representation here.
Imogen is a character who smashes stereotypes with her powerful sense of humour and cutting remarks and I adored her for it.
So many young autistic women (and this *slightly* older one) will read this and recognise themselves in the pages of this book and I cannot wait to see what Anna Zoe Quirke writes next.
