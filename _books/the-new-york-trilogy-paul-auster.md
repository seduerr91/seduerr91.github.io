## Summary

### The New York Trilogy

#### Introduction

Paul Auster’s *The New York Trilogy* is a seminal work of postmodern fiction that deconstructs the conventions of the detective novel. While the stories begin with the familiar hallmarks of crime fiction-mysterious phone calls, missing persons, and surveillance-they quickly pivot to deeper, metaphysical inquiries. Auster uses the genre not to solve crimes, but to explore the nature of language, the fragility of identity, and the elusive search for meaning in a chaotic world. The trilogy, comprising *City of Glass*, *Ghosts*, and *The Locked Room*, is less about "whodunnit" and more about the existential crisis of the detective.

#### The Detective as a Metaphor

In these three novellas, the figure of the detective serves as a metaphor for the human attempt to extract meaning from the world. Auster treats the detective novel as a form to tell stories about the struggle to live with ambiguity. The act of detecting becomes a process of self-interpretation. The characters are not necessarily solving external mysteries, but are instead engaged in a quest to find their own identities.

- **Epistemological Quests:** The book questions the very process of researching things and drawing a larger truth from collected symbols. The detective element serves as a "metaphor for the way we create meaning in everyday life".
- **Semiotics and Signs:** The trilogy functions as a "treaty on semiotics," exploring how we create our selves and our perceptions through the symbols we use to navigate the world.

#### City of Glass: The Dissolved Self

The first and longest story, *City of Glass*, begins with a wrong number. Daniel Quinn, a writer of detective fiction who is grieving the loss of his wife and son, receives a call asking for the "Paul Auster Detective Agency." Intrigued, Quinn assumes the identity of Paul Auster the detective and takes a case from a man named Peter Stillman, who fears his recently released father. As Quinn shadows Stillman senior through the streets of New York, he begins to lose his own grip on reality. He creates maps that he believes are secret messages and tries to decode the madman's language, eventually forfeiting his own identity to the process. The story illustrates the danger of obsession and the way a role can consume the actor.

> "New York was an inexhaustible space, a labyrinth of endless steps... Lost, not only in the city, but within himself as well."

#### Ghosts: The Watcher and the Watched

The second installment, *Ghosts*, follows a private detective named Blue who is hired by a man named White to shadow a man named Black. Blue sets up an apartment across from Black's residence and settles in for a long vigil. However, nothing suspicious happens. As days turn into months, Blue’s boredom turns into introspection and mimicry-if Black reads a book, Blue reads the same book. The investigation ceases to be about Black and becomes entirely about Blue’s own existence. The story highlights the claustrophobic nature of surveillance and how the act of watching can isolate the watcher from the world of action.

#### The Locked Room: The Literary Legacy

The final story, *The Locked Room*, is told by a narrator who is contacted by the wife of his childhood friend, Fanshawe, who has disappeared. Fanshawe has left behind a cache of brilliant writings. The narrator steps into Fanshawe’s life, publishing the work, marrying his wife, and adopting his son. Eventually, the narrator embarks on a quest to find the "real" Fanshawe, interviewing those who knew him. This story pulls the perspective up further, questioning the nature of biography and the ownership of another’s life. It ultimately suggests that the narrator himself may be a creation of Fanshawe’s, blurring the lines between author, character, and reader.

#### Identity and Existentialism

At the core of the trilogy is the theme of identity. The characters are often writers or detectives-professions that rely on observation and interpretation-and they frequently find that the more they observe others, the less they understand themselves.

- **The Opaque Self:** As Auster writes, "We exist for ourselves, perhaps, and at times we even have a glimmer of who we are, but in the end, we can never be sure, and as our lives go on, we become more and more opaque to ourselves."
- **Adopted Personas:** Characters like Quinn willingly shed their histories, suggesting that identity is fluid and perhaps non-existent when subjected to the pressures of a specific role or obsession.

#### The Architecture of New York

New York City is more than a backdrop in these stories; it is a labyrinth that reflects the characters' internal states. The city is depicted as a maze of endless steps where characters wander to escape the obligation to think. The urban landscape, with its history and architecture, serves as a physical manifestation of the mind’s complexity. Auster includes references to literary figures like Poe and Whitman, grounding the metaphysical wandering in the concrete reality of the city’s streets.

#### The Illusion of Resolution

Perhaps the most defining characteristic of the trilogy is its refusal to provide closure. Each story ends on an ambiguous note, frustrating readers who expect the traditional resolution of a mystery. There are no answers to the questions raised, only more questions.

- **Unanswered Questions:** The protagonists’ quests invariably lead to dead ends, disappearances, or realizations of futility.
- **Life as Chance:** The stories posit that "each life is no more than the sum of contingent facts, a chronicle of chance intersections, of flukes, of random events that divulge nothing but their own lack of purpose."

This lack of resolution is not a flaw but a thematic statement. It suggests that the desire for a neat narrative arc is a human construct that reality cannot support. By denying the reader the satisfaction of a solution, Auster forces them to inhabit the same state of uncertainty and ambiguity as his characters.

#### Conclusion

*The New York Trilogy* is a masterful exploration of the boundaries between fact and fiction, detective and writer, and the self and the other. It uses the engaging shell of a detective story to deliver a challenging meditation on the human condition. While it may be the "least fun detective novel" one could read, it is a profound study of how we create meaning in a world that offers none. Ultimately, the trilogy suggests that the only mystery worth solving is the mystery of oneself, even if that mystery remains forever unsolved.

   mookseandgripes.com · 2008-10-05
   Thankfully, I can (sort of) say that I’ve read *three* of Auster’s books as this is a “trilogy.”
Here we have three pieces: “City of Glass” (1985), “Ghosts” (1986), and “The Locked Room” (1986).
The three were compiled almost right away (which was the point all along) into a single work: *The New York Trilogy*.
Indeed, even while they were being written they were referred to as The New York Trilogy.
While all three are unique, each also ties to themes and even passages of another.
I’d go so far as to say that in order to fully enjoy them, they should each be read and in order (and maybe read again).
...
Let me first give a brief introduction to each story.
Here’s my feeble attempt at a compelling blurb!
**City of Glass**: The first story is also the longest, coming in at 130 pages in my edition.
Daniel Quinn is a mystery writer who lost his wife and young son a few years ago in an accident.
Lonely, he almost feels more at home in his pen-name William Wilson, or even in his character’s persona.
One late night, he gets a phone call for a Paul Auster, the detective.
Quinn tells the caller he’s not the man, but then he wishes he’d have played along for a while.
Fortunately, he gets his chance.
Assuming the identity of Paul Auster, Quinn accepts a strange assignment involving metaphysical elements from Genesis, Don Quixote, Humpty-Dumpty, and more.
The questions we ask are pulled up from the level of the story itself to the nature of story, the writing of it, etc.
**Ghosts**: Blue is a detective, trained by Brown.
White hires Blue to spy on Black.
Blue knows next to nothing about what he’s looking for, so he sets up shop in an apartment across the street from Black’s apartment in Brooklyn.
Not much happens — on the outside, anyway.
However, Blue is not necessarily bored.
On the contrary, he comes to know himself better than before.
**The Locked Room**: The first-person narrator in this story is contacted by Sophie, the wife of Fanshawe, his best friend during childhood (I caught the references to Hawthorne, but I don’t know what they mean).
Fanshawe has disappeared, months ago, in fact.
It’s so uncharacteristic of him that the narrator and Sophie assume he’s dead.
It’s been years since the narrator had any contact with Fanshawe, and he feels he doesn’t know him at all anymore.
Nevertheless, shortly before he disappeared Fanshawe gave his wife all of his writings and told him to contact the narrator if something should happen to him.
Turns out that the writings are brilliant, and the narrator and Sophie are falling in love.
The strange thing about the blurbs above is that they sound sort of like hard-boiled detective novels.
That’s not the case though.
The basics of the plot intentionally play with elements from classic crime, but these books dwell on the metaphysical.
And Auster’s writing is at once clear and poetic (not always the case when dealing with books that ask questions beyond their capacity to answer).
Interestingly, just when I thought “City of Glass” and “Ghosts” were already working layers and layers above the actual stories, “The Locked Room” pulls the perspective up even further, leaving more questions.
So, how does New York play into this?
First and foremost, that’s where the stories are set, for the most part.
But there’s more to it, especially in “City of Glass.”
>
New York was an inexhaustible space, a labyrinth of endless steps, and no matter how far he walked, no matter how well he came to know its neighborhoods and streets, it always left him with the feeling of being lost.
Lost, not only in the city, but within himself as well.
Each time he took a walk, he felt as though he were leaving himself behind, and by giving himself up to the movement of the streets, by reducing himself to a seeing eye, he was able to escape the obligation to think, and this, more than anything else, brought him a measure of peace, a salutary emptiness within.
Besides presenting the city as a sort of motif within which to explore metaphysical ideas, Auster also uses this opportunity to present some of New York’s fascinating history.
For example, stories about where Poe, Witman, Thoreau, and others sat and pondered, are slyly inserted into the trilogy, shedding light on the mood while presenting more questions.
Tales about the architecture, in particular about the construction of the Brooklyn Bridge, are inserted in ways that both give a sense of place and a sense of the psychology of the character.
...
Indeed, and I don’t think it’s a spoiler to say this, the answer to all of the questions I came up with were not answered.
At the end of each story I found myself asking, “What about *this*?
What about *that*?”
Don’t most mystery novels tie up the loose ends?
Is Auster just fooling with me?
Believe me, though, I was grinning while I was asking those questions.
These stories are unsatisfying in the most *satisfying* way.
Where most mysteries end up falling short of the reader’s expectations when full disclosure is made, these excellent pieces allow the reader to keep on searching.
