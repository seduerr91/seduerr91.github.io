I want to become a pilot in `2027`.

Right now, I only have the FAA drone certificate, which is a real start but obviously not the same thing as flying an airplane. The good news is that it is not starting from zero. I already have some weather, airspace, planning, and safety context. The next step is turning that into a real airplane training plan that fits western Washington instead of pretending I live in Arizona.

This roadmap assumes my goal is a **Private Pilot Certificate, Airplane Single-Engine Land**, not an airline path yet. That is the right first milestone: it gets me into the cockpit, lets me carry passengers, and creates the foundation for instrument, commercial, or anything else later.

## What the FAA Path Actually Looks Like

The rough FAA sequence is:

1. Student pilot certificate
2. Medical certificate
3. Ground study
4. Flight training
5. Knowledge test
6. Solo
7. Cross-country and checkride prep
8. Practical test

For a private pilot certificate, the FAA minimum is `40` flight hours, but the FAA also notes that most people actually take closer to `60` to `75` hours. I should plan for the real number, not the legal minimum.

I also need at least a **third-class medical certificate before my first solo flight**, and the private pilot airplane knowledge test is the **PAR** exam: `60` questions, `2.0` hours, and a `70%` passing score.

## Why Washington Changes the Plan

If I lived somewhere dry and predictable, I could try to speed-run the whole thing in one season. Western Washington is not that place.

That means the smarter plan is:

- Use fall and winter for theory, reading, systems, weather, charts, and FAA paperwork
- Use spring to begin or ramp up actual training
- Use summer for the heaviest flight block
- Use early fall as checkride buffer in case weather, maintenance, or scheduling gets in the way

That is not slower. It is just more realistic.

## Phase 1: Late 2026, Make Aviation Real

Before I buy a pile of books and equipment, I want to make the goal feel concrete.

### Things I Should Do First

- Book `1` discovery flight with a **CFI** at a school I might actually train with
- Ask my pilot uncle-in-law to take me on `1-2` flights in his Cessna so I can feel the environment, radios, checklists, and cockpit rhythm
- Decide which airport and school I would realistically use for regular training
- Start a running note with questions, fears, costs, and logistics so I stop keeping the whole plan vague

The important nuance is that a flight with my uncle is great for motivation and exposure, but it is not a substitute for training unless he is also the instructor giving the lesson.

## Phase 2: Winter 2026 to Early 2027, Ground School Season

Winter is the right time for the academic side.

This is when I want to build understanding in:

- Airspace
- Regulations
- Weather theory and weather products
- Aircraft systems
- Aerodynamics
- Flight instruments
- Weight and balance
- Performance
- Sectional charts
- Navigation
- Airport operations
- Risk management and aeronautical decision-making

This is also where the gap between drone knowledge and airplane knowledge becomes obvious.

### What My Drone Certificate Already Helps With

- Basic weather awareness
- Airspace structure
- TFR and NOTAM habits
- Safety mindset
- Preflight planning discipline

### What Will Be New

- Aircraft systems and limitations
- Weight and balance in a real aircraft
- Takeoff and landing performance
- Radio work from inside the cockpit
- Sectional navigation and pilotage
- Night operations
- Emergency procedures
- Human factors and aeromedical issues
- The feel of flying the airplane, not just managing rules around it

If I try to study everything at once, I will just create a pile of tabs and feel productive without actually progressing.

A better order is:

1. Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
2. Aviation Weather Handbook
3. Chart Users' Guide
4. Airplane Flying Handbook
5. Private Pilot ACS
6. Testing supplement and sample PAR questions

### The Highest-Value PHAK Chapters for Me

- Aeronautical Decision-Making

## Phase 3: Administrative Setup Before Solo Matters

There are two pieces of FAA admin I should not leave until the last minute.

### 1. Student Pilot Certificate

I should create an **IACRA** account and start the student pilot certificate process early. The student certificate is now separate from the medical and does not expire.

### 2. Third-Class Medical

I should create a **MedXPress** account and schedule an appointment with an **AME** early enough that I am not blocked when I am ready to solo.

If there is any reason at all to suspect a medical complication, I would rather learn that early than after spending a lot of money on flight training.

## Phase 4: Spring 2027, Start Flying for Real

Once the weather gets better, the goal should shift from "aviation interest" to "consistent training."

The most important thing here is frequency.

Flying once every two or three weeks is a great way to spend money relearning the same things. If possible, I want something closer to:

- `2` flights per week minimum
- `3` flights per week if schedule and money allow

### What Spring Training Will Likely Cover

- Preflight inspection
- Checklists
- Taxi work
- Normal takeoffs and landings
- Climbs, descents, and turns
- Slow flight
- Power-on and power-off stalls
- Ground reference maneuvers
- Traffic patterns
- Emergency procedures

This is the phase where I stop thinking like a person who is "interested in becoming a pilot" and start behaving like a student pilot.

## Phase 5: Late Spring to Summer 2027, Solo and Written

This should be the heart of the year.

### Summer Goals

- Be ready for first solo
- Pass the PAR knowledge test
- Start cross-country dual flights
- Build comfort with radios, towered airports, and navigation

By this point I want the ground school material to be mostly behind me so I can focus on executing instead of constantly feeling academically underwater.

A strong target would be:

- **By June 2027:** first solo and written test passed

## Phase 6: Summer to Early Fall 2027, Cross-Country and Checkride Prep

This is where the training starts to feel like actual flying rather than only lesson blocks near the airport.

### This Phase Likely Includes

- Dual cross-country flights
- Night training
- Instrument reference time
- Solo cross-country requirements
- Towered-airport solo work
- Checkride prep flights
- Oral exam prep using the ACS

This is also where western Washington summer becomes a real advantage. I want to use the best weather window for the biggest training chunk.

## How I Want to Learn Aircraft Better

I do not only want to pass the written. I want to actually understand the machine.

A good practical mix would be:

- Read the aircraft systems chapters in the PHAK
- Read enough of the AMT handbooks to understand airframe, engine, and basic maintenance concepts
- Ask my instructor to walk me through the POH and systems in detail
- Spend time around preflights, oil checks, fuel sampling, tie-downs, and postflight habits
- If possible, sit in on a maintenance conversation or annual inspection discussion just to see how owners think about airplane care

I do not need an A&P license. I just want more mechanical intuition.

## Final Take

The path from drone pilot to airplane pilot is not small, but it is also not mysterious.

For me, the smartest version of this plan is:

- Use winter for theory
- Use a couple of early flights to confirm I love it
- Handle the medical and student certificate early
- Train consistently in spring and summer
- Aim for the private pilot checkride in late `2027`

The key is not trying to do everything immediately. The key is turning "I want to become a pilot" into a seasonal plan with specific milestones.

That makes it feel less like a dream and more like a project.
