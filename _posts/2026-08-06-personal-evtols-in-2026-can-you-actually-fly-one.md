Watching videos about personal flying vehicles makes it seem like we will all be commuting through the sky within a few years.

The reality is more nuanced.

As of August 6, 2026, some of these aircraft are real products with paying customers. Others are production prototypes taking deposits. And a few are still ambitious promises wearing very polished marketing.

If I were evaluating one today, I would care about four questions:

- can I actually buy it?
- how far can I really fly it?
- what regulations apply?
- is it useful beyond being an expensive toy?

## The Short Answer

Yes, you can actually fly one today.

But in most cases, what you can fly today looks much more like **recreational aviation** than **personal transportation**.

The biggest dividing line is not just battery technology. It is regulation.

## First: What Can You Legally Do?

In the U.S., several of these aircraft are designed to fit under [FAA Part 103 ultralight rules](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-103).

That matters a lot.

Part 103 is why aircraft like Jetson ONE, Pivotal Helix, and LIFT HEXA can be flown without a pilot certificate in the U.S. The FAA explicitly notes that you do **not** need a pilot license to fly an ultralight vehicle that qualifies under Part 103.

But that freedom comes with very real limits:

- no passengers
- recreation or sport purposes only
- no flight over congested areas or open-air assemblies of people
- no Class A, B, C, or D airspace without prior authorization
- visual, fair-weather style operations rather than all-weather transport

So when marketing implies, "Fly from your driveway to the office," the practical answer is usually: **not legally, not routinely, and not the way people imagine.**

Think of most Part 103 eVTOLs as recreational aircraft, not commuting vehicles.

## 1. RTOR / Rictor X4

This is the aircraft that made a lot of people stop scrolling.

At roughly `$39,900`, it sits in a completely different psychological category from the rest of this list. That is still a lot of money, but it is no longer "supercar for the sky" money.

The problem is not the idea. The problem is certainty.

This is the least mature and least proven option here. Even the naming and published details feel less settled than the rest of the field. If it ships as promised and truly qualifies under Part 103, it could become the most disruptive product in the category. But that is still a very big "if."

My reaction is simple:

- amazing headline price
- intriguing form factor
- highest execution risk on the list

## 2. Jetson ONE

Jetson is probably the aircraft that made personal eVTOL feel real to the public.

And unlike many startups, it is not just a render company anymore. It has delivered aircraft, it has a visible order book, and its public order page now shows entire 2026 and 2027 production sold out with estimated delivery in 2028 for new orders.

This is the closest thing to a flying motorcycle on the market:

- open cockpit
- short endurance
- high grin factor
- very little illusion that this is a substitute for a car

That honesty is part of the appeal.

If what you want is **joy, short hops, and spectacle**, Jetson ONE is probably the most fun answer on the list.

## 3. AIR ONE

This is where things become genuinely interesting.

AIR ONE is not a Part 103 toy. It is a much more serious aircraft:

- `2` seats
- up to `1` hour endurance
- `60-100` mile published range
- fast cruise around `115 kts`
- private pilot license required

This is much closer to owning a small airplane than owning a recreational ultralight.

That matters because the mission changes. Instead of hovering around a field for 10 or 15 miles, AIR ONE starts to look capable of actual regional transportation, at least on paper and assuming certification and operations mature the way the company intends.

If I were looking for the first personal eVTOL that might evolve into something truly practical, this would be near the top of the list.

## 4. Pivotal Helix

If I had to choose the product that feels the most real in the U.S. market right now, this is probably it.

Pivotal is selling the Helix commercially in the U.S., publishes real pricing, includes training, and operates inside the Part 103 framework. That combination matters more than futuristic marketing.

The Helix is also a useful reminder that maturity often looks less dramatic than hype:

- `1` seat
- `20` minutes
- `20` miles max range
- no pilot license required

That is not a commuting revolution.

It is a real aircraft that people can actually buy and fly.

And in early markets, that often matters more than big concept promises.

## 5. LIFT HEXA

HEXA is one of my favorite examples of a company understanding the market it can actually serve.

LIFT is not pretending this is mass commuting. Today it is focused much more on flight experiences and controlled use cases than on retail ownership. Its own FAQ says it is **not currently offering aircraft for sale to the general public**.

That makes it less relevant if your question is, "What can I buy for my garage?"

But it makes it very relevant if your question is, "What can I actually experience today?"

HEXA feels like:

- the cleanest "go try this now" option
- a great fit for tourism and first-response experimentation
- a weak fit for everyday ownership

## 6. Alef Model A

Alef is still the most science-fiction object on this list.

It is also the one that maps most cleanly onto the public fantasy of a flying car:

- it drives on roads
- it fits into a regular lane
- it fits in a regular parking space and garage
- it promises vertical takeoff and flight over traffic

That is why it gets so much attention.

It also means the certification and execution burden is enormous.

Alef clearly deserves credit for getting to real prototypes and test authorization. But I would still file it under **promising, fascinating, and far from routine ownership**.

## 7. Doroni H1-X

Doroni is the one I would watch most closely as a future-looking "personal air car" play.

Its public specs are compelling:

- `2` seats
- `100` mile target range
- `120 mph` top speed
- enclosed cabin
- ducted-fan design
- strong consumer-friendly branding

The current pre-order flow now points to an **expected price of `$400,000`** and estimated deliveries beginning in 2028.

That tells you almost everything you need to know.

It is not a "buy it and fly it now" product. It is a bet on certification, execution, and patience.

## Could You Actually Use One Around the Bay Area?

This is where the dream gets filtered through reality.

Suppose you live in San Jose.

| Destination | Distance | On-Paper Read |
|---|---:|---|
| Palo Alto | `18 mi` | Jetson and Helix are borderline; AIR ONE is comfortable; Alef and Doroni depend on targets becoming reality |
| Santa Cruz | `30 mi` | AIR ONE is the first clearly plausible option; Alef and Doroni only if targets and certification land |
| San Francisco | `48 mi` | AIR ONE looks plausible on paper; Alef and Doroni remain target-dependent |
| Monterey | `70 mi` | AIR ONE is the only near-term entrant that starts to look credible here |
| Lake Tahoe | `180 mi` | None of these are really the answer |

And even this table is generous.

The bigger limitation is not just battery. It is airspace, safe landing options, weather, certification, reserve planning, and the fact that Part 103 aircraft are not allowed to operate like personal commuter helicopters over dense urban areas.

## Final Thoughts

Personal eVTOLs in 2026 are real.

But they are not all real in the same way.

Some are:

- actual products with real pricing and real deliveries
- recreational aircraft with strict legal operating limits
- thrilling but short-range machines

Others are:

- pre-order programs
- certification stories
- future transportation bets

To me, that is still exciting.

We are no longer in the era where personal electric aircraft exist only in concept videos. You can now point to real aircraft, real customers, and real operating rules.

The missing piece is not imagination anymore.

It is the long, slow work of certification, infrastructure, battery improvement, and fitting these machines into the real world.
