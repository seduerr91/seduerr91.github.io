I have taken a West Coast Swing class before. That is both reassuring and slightly frustrating.

I am not starting from zero: somewhere in my body are the rhythm, the slot, the anchor, and a few basic patterns. But enough time has passed that they no longer arrive automatically. I can remember *having known* the dance more easily than I can remember how to dance it.

That changes the goal. I do not need to collect dozens of new moves. I need to make the fundamentals feel familiar again, then reconnect them to a partner and to music.

This is my plan for doing that without turning YouTube into a substitute for actually dancing.

## What I Am Trying to Recover

West Coast Swing is a slotted partner dance. One partner travels mainly along an imaginary line—the slot—while the other creates space, redirects movement, and then reestablishes the connection. The roles are called leader and follower, and either person can learn either role.

The basic rhythmic vocabulary is smaller than the list of named patterns makes it appear:

- a **walk** takes one beat;
- a **triple step** takes two beats and sounds like `step-step-step` or `1 & 2`;
- most beginner patterns use either six or eight counts;
- an **anchor** usually occupies the last triple and finishes the current pattern before the next one begins.

A common six-count rhythm is:

**walk, walk, triple step, anchor step**<br>
**1, 2, 3 & 4, 5 & 6**

The basic eight-count whip adds two walking beats:

**walk, walk, triple step, walk, walk, anchor step**<br>
**1, 2, 3 & 4, 5, 6, 7 & 8**

Those counts describe rhythm, not one universal sequence of foot placements. The feet and direction change with the role and pattern. I want to recover the rhythm first, then let a teacher help clean up the exact mechanics.

## The Five Things to Relearn First

### 1. Finding the Beat and Changing Weight

Before practicing a pattern, I should be able to walk to the beat without looking at my feet. Then I can practice compact triple steps in place: left-right-left, right-left-right, repeating while my full weight arrives on every step.

This is more important than it sounds. A rushed `&` count, a partial weight change, or feet that spread too far apart will make every later pattern harder.

My first solo drill is five minutes long:

1. Clap or walk the main beat for one song.
2. Alternate two walks and two triple steps.
3. Say `walk, walk, tri-ple-step, an-chor-step` out loud.
4. Keep the steps underneath me and my upper body quiet.
5. Repeat on both starting feet.

If the rhythm survives while I look forward and relax my shoulders, the old coordination is returning.

### 2. The Slot

The slot gives West Coast Swing its shape. The follower normally travels through it; the leader should move out of the way rather than block it or steer a partner around with the arms.

I can place a strip of painter's tape on the floor and practice sending or traveling straight along it. This will expose wide, wandering steps immediately. Small steps make timing, balance, and redirection easier.

### 3. The Anchor

The anchor is not just three decorative steps at the end. It finishes the pattern, creates space, and develops the stretch that makes the next lead or follow readable.

I want to feel three ideas:

- I complete my own weight changes instead of leaning on my partner.
- My arm stays responsive rather than rigid or floppy.
- I do not rush forward into the next pattern before the connection invites it.

The anchor deserves its own practice because it appears again and again. If it feels calm, the whole dance starts to feel calmer.

### 4. Three Core Pattern Families

I only need three families to get back onto a social floor:

| Pattern | Counts | What it teaches |
|---|---:|---|
| Sugar push, or push break | 6 | Moving from stretch into compression and back out without shoving or pulling |
| Left and right side passes, including the underarm pass | 6 | Keeping the slot clear, traveling cleanly, and changing sides |
| Basic whip | 8 | Switching from six- to eight-count timing, redirection, and rotation |

The [West Coast Swing Online basic-pattern guide](https://www.westcoastswingonline.com/west-coast-swing-basic-patterns/) groups the dance in a similar way. Once these families feel comfortable, a sugar tuck, tuck turn, inside turn, or whip variation is easier to understand as a variation rather than a completely new piece of choreography.

### 5. Connection Before Styling

West Coast Swing is a conversation carried through body movement and a light physical connection. The hands transmit information, but they should not manufacture the dance by pulling, lifting, or twisting a partner's arm.

Good early questions are:

- Can I maintain my own balance?
- Does movement begin in my body before it reaches the hand?
- Can my partner finish the current pattern and anchor?
- Am I creating compression or stretch together with my partner, rather than forcing it alone?
- Can I reduce the effort and still make the idea clear?

That last question matters. Muscling through a pattern can disguise bad timing when I dance with an accommodating partner. Light, clear connection reveals what I actually know.

## My Progressive Video Refresher

I want these videos to function like a short curriculum, not an endless feed. I will watch one section, stand up, practice it, and return to a real class before assuming the screen has taught me partner connection.

### Stage 1: See the Whole Map

Start with [How to Dance the West Coast Swing Basic Steps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dfVpwMLqm-o) by West Coast Swing Online. It teaches the sugar push, side passes, and basic whip, with chapter markers for each. On the first viewing, I only need to remember what each pattern looks and sounds like.

The longer [West Coast Swing Basic Steps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cKcamMuk3sA) video adds the sugar tuck, traveling tuck, and inside roll. I will save those additions until the first three patterns are steady.

### Stage 2: Rebuild Solo Footwork

Next, use [How to Practice West Coast Swing Alone](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D12lAQ0_0Sc). It moves from footwork drills into basic-pattern footwork and then spin practice. The footwork and pattern sections are my priority; spins can wait until I stay balanced and on time.

An alternative is the follow-along [West Coast Swing solo line dance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mArUlxB-fJY). It is useful when isolated drills become boring and I need a way to keep moving through an entire song.

### Stage 3: Add a Partner and Connection

Then watch [West Coast Swing Connection for Beginners](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0vO6R7YuRcw). This is the point where I need a partner, group class, or private lesson. Stretch and compression are shared sensations; I cannot reliably learn them by pulling on a door handle and guessing.

I will use video here to supply questions for a teacher, not to certify my own technique.

### Stage 4: Add Music Without Losing the Basics

Only after the rhythm and connection are stable will I use [West Coast Swing Musicality: Accenting the Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qBb2p8yGvwU). The goal is initially modest: hear a phrase change, pause or simplify on an accent, and preserve the partnership.

For a newer overview, [Master Musicality for West Coast Swing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MmUxfKFX88s) explores how to connect familiar movement to musical changes. I do not need advanced syncopations yet. Clean basics that respond to the music will feel better than complicated patterns that miss it.

Thibault and Nicole Ramirez also offer a more recent [complete-beginner series introduction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JriglXKDjfM), with the structured playlist linked from the description. That is a good second teaching perspective if one explanation does not click, but I should avoid mixing five different systems in the same practice session.

## A Four-Week Return Plan

### Week 1: Rhythm and Balance

Three times this week, I will do 15 minutes of solo practice:

- walk to the beat;
- alternate walks and triple steps;
- practice anchors on both feet;
- trace the slot on the floor;
- record 30 seconds from the front and side.

Success means I change weight clearly, stay over my feet, and keep moving when I stop saying the counts aloud.

### Week 2: The Three Pattern Families

I will review the sugar push, both side passes, the underarm pass, and basic whip. If I have a willing partner, we will practice slowly and stop whenever the connection becomes a tug-of-war.

Success means I can name whether I am in a push, pass, or whip family and finish the anchor without immediately launching another move.

### Week 3: Return to Class

This is the most important week. I will take a beginner or fundamentals class even if I once took a higher level. I will tell the instructor that I am returning after a long break and want feedback on timing, anchor, and connection.

One precise correction from a teacher is worth hours of repeating the wrong movement at home.

### Week 4: Class Plus Social Dancing

I will attend a class followed by a social, if possible, and stay for at least five dances. I can ask partners, “Would you like to dance?” and mention that I am getting back into it. Either person can decline a dance, and either person can end one if something feels unsafe.

My aim is not to impress anyone. It is to keep time, make the basics comfortable, adapt to different partners, and say thank you after each song.

## A Sustainable Weekly Rhythm After That

The best ongoing formula is simple:

- **one class** for instruction and correction;
- **one social** for adaptability and confidence;
- **two short solo sessions** for timing, footwork, and balance;
- **one video concept at a time** rather than a queue of unused ideas.

After each class, I will write down only three things: the pattern, the technique point, and the mistake I most want to avoid. Within 24 hours, I will practice that material for ten minutes. That small recall session will do more for memory than rewatching the entire lesson next month.

## The Mistakes I Expect to Make

Returning dancers often remember the outline of a pattern before its timing and connection. I should watch for:

- taking steps that are too large;
- staring at my feet;
- treating the anchor as an optional flourish;
- pulling with the arms when I am late;
- rushing through compression in the sugar push;
- blocking the slot during a pass;
- adding turns before I can keep six- and eight-count timing straight;
- apologizing through the whole dance instead of resetting and continuing.

Mistakes are not evidence that the old class was wasted. They are the map of what needs refreshing.

## What Progress Should Feel Like

My first milestone is not a flashy video. It is one relaxed song made mostly from sugar pushes, side passes, an underarm pass, and a basic whip. I stay on time, my partner has room to move, neither of us needs force, and I recover when something goes wrong.

Then I can add a tuck turn, one clean footwork variation, better phrasing, and more play. The vocabulary can expand gradually because the conversation underneath it already works.

I have learned this dance once. I do not need to prove that by jumping ahead. I need to give my body enough good repetitions to recognize the rhythm again.

**Status: finding the slot, finishing the anchor, and getting back onto the floor.**
